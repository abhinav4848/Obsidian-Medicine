## Current style preferences inferred from edits
- Use natural, first-person language with contractions where they fit: “I've been trying”, “I'd had one session”, “once I'm at base surgery”.
- Use direct, everyday expressions such as “get up to speed”, “sit in on” and “start work on”, rather than formal portfolio language such as “make my progress visible” or “arrange submission”.
- Describe what was actually discussed, what the user tried, what happened and what they plan next. Include concrete details supplied by the user rather than filling space with general statements about feedback, reflection or competence.
- Keep practical barriers and the user's efforts visible. Explain the circumstances plainly, without inventing fault, excuses or a successful outcome.
- Be accurate about progress: an introductory PSW session is not evidence of communication improvement; starting a QIP is different from submitting it.
- Use familiar training abbreviations without unnecessary expansion, including TPD, CEPS, CCRs, WPBAs, PSW, QIP, UUC, ESR and CSR. Preserve supplied terms such as TALC without guessing what they mean.
- Prefer short narrative paragraphs for the discussion and reflection, and practical bullets for learning needs. Avoid adding generic commitments simply to populate every part of “maintain, improve or stop”.
- Make actions specific to the user's circumstances, such as swapping study days or aiming to finish the portfolio by the end of December, when those details have been supplied.
- Keep grammar readable in new drafts without deliberately reproducing typos such as “Event though”. Preserve the user's edited text when saving it; ask or wait for a request before polishing that version.
- Explain why a topic mattered personally, connecting it to current support, resources and exam preparation when supplied. Use the user's tentative phrasing where appropriate, such as “I think”, “I will try” and “occasionally”, so commitments remain proportionate.
- Include concrete accounts of discussion and patient examples when supplied, rather than generic statements that a session was useful. Keep examples non-identifying and distinguish discussion about MDT input from an MDT that actually took place.
- Use the correct person or service for follow-up: the user changed “my supervisor” to “my PSW” for consultation-skills learning. Do not assume these are interchangeable.
- Respect deletions. Do not automatically restore generic feedback requests, continuity plans or other commitments the user removed from an entry. A deletion in one entry does not establish a universal objection to that topic.
- In capability justifications, explain explicitly why the capability matters to the user's current development, alongside the evidence from the event. Ground this in supplied circumstances such as PSW, TALC and SCA preparation.
- When explaining a capability link, name its central activity plainly: for example, “Working on communicating and consulting.” Avoid leaving the relationship implicit in generic reflection language.
- Give practice context a clear role in the explanation: show how the patient population affected the teaching topic or approach. Preserve context-specific clinical reflections without turning them into universal treatment rules.
