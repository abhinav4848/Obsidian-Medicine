## Highest-priority clinical corrections

### 1. Severe hypoglycaemia: the 10% glucose option is too small
**Note:** [[hypoglycemia#Treatment|hypoglycemia — Treatment]], lines 37–38.
The unconscious-patient options include **50 mL of 10% glucose**, which supplies only **5 g glucose**. JBDS's adult hospital algorithm uses **200 mL of 10% glucose or 100 mL of 20% glucose over 15 minutes**, followed by a glucose recheck after 10 minutes and repeat treatment if needed. The 75 mL of 20% option supplies 15 g but also differs from that algorithm. This is an adult protocol; children's doses differ. [JBDS hypoglycaemia algorithm in the Front Door guideline, Appendix 2](https://www.diabetes.org.uk/sites/default/files/2023-06/JBDS%2016%20Diabetes%20at%20the%20Front%20Door%20Guideline%20qr%20code.pdf).

### 2. Do not give a general instruction to stop basal insulin after a hypo
**Note:** [[hypoglycemia#Treatment|hypoglycemia — Treatment]], line 45.
The instruction to reduce/stop long-acting insulin needs a **type 1 diabetes exception**. Basal insulin must not simply be omitted in type 1 diabetes; review and adjust the regimen, with monitoring, to avoid subsequent DKA. [JBDS enteral feeding guideline, hypoglycaemia safety notes](https://www.diabetes.org.uk/sites/default/files/2024-04/JBDS%2005%20Enteral%20Feeding%20Guideline%20April%202024.pdf).

### 3. DKA: add glucose alongside saline, rather than switching to 5% glucose at 12
**Note:** [[diabetic ketoacidosis#Treatment|diabetic ketoacidosis — Treatment]], line 27.
The adult JBDS pathway adds **10% glucose at 125 mL/hour when glucose falls below 14 mmol/L**, alongside the saline replacement that is still needed. Continue insulin to clear ketosis; consider reducing its rate to 0.05 units/kg/hour at this point. Glucose normalisation alone does not mean DKA has resolved. [JBDS DKA guideline, treatment pathway and 60-minute-to-6-hour management](https://www.diabetes.org.uk/sites/default/files/2023-03/JBDS%2002%20DKA%20Guideline%20with%20qr%20code.pdf).

### 4. DKA with hypotension: initial boluses should include reassessment
**Note:** [[diabetic ketoacidosis#Treatment|diabetic ketoacidosis — Treatment]], line 18.
The blanket **1 litre over 10–15 minutes** differs from JBDS's initial **500 mL 0.9% saline over 10–15 minutes**, repeated if systolic BP remains below 90 mmHg while seeking senior input. Reassess response and other causes of shock; age and cardiac/renal disease affect fluid tolerance. [JBDS DKA guideline, systolic BP below 90 mmHg](https://www.diabetes.org.uk/sites/default/files/2023-03/JBDS%2002%20DKA%20Guideline%20with%20qr%20code.pdf).

### 5. Hyperkalaemia: a one-week repeat is unsafe as a blanket rule
**Note:** [[hyperkalemia#Treatment|hyperkalemia — Treatment]], line 36.
For an **unexpected community result**, UKKA recommends repeat potassium within **3 days for 5.5–5.9**, within **1 day for 6.0–6.4 mmol/L**, and urgent hospital assessment for **≥6.5 mmol/L**. Acute illness/AKI can warrant hospital assessment even below 6.5. The note's “≤6.0 … in a week” particularly mishandles a result of exactly 6.0. [UKKA July 2026 guideline, recommendations 1.2.1–1.2.3 and 4.1–4.2](https://www.ukkidney.org/sites/default/files/documents/FINAL%20VERSION%20-%20UKKA%20CLINICAL%20PRACTICE%20GUIDELINE%20-%20MANAGEMENT%20OF%20HYPERKALAEMIA%20IN%20ADULTS%20-%20UPDATED%20JULY%202026_0.pdf).

### 6. Hyperkalaemia: calcium and Calcium Resonium advice need correction
**Note:** [[hyperkalemia#Treatment|hyperkalemia — Treatment]], line 38.
UKKA's IV calcium recommendation is for **ECG changes from hyperkalaemia**, rather than automatically for every severe potassium result. Severe hyperkalaemia still requires urgent potassium-lowering treatment. **Calcium Resonium is no longer recommended routinely for acute hyperkalaemia**; the modern pathway includes newer binders. A normal ECG does not make severe hyperkalaemia safe. [UKKA July 2026 guideline, recommendations 16.2 and 16.6](https://www.ukkidney.org/sites/default/files/documents/FINAL%20VERSION%20-%20UKKA%20CLINICAL%20PRACTICE%20GUIDELINE%20-%20MANAGEMENT%20OF%20HYPERKALAEMIA%20IN%20ADULTS%20-%20UPDATED%20JULY%202026_0.pdf).

### 7. Hyponatraemia below 125: do not automatically wait two weeks
**Note:** [[hyponatremia#Classification of severity|hyponatremia — Classification of severity]], line 32.
“Na <125 & no symptoms, repeat in 2 weeks” is too broad. Assess promptly for severity, trajectory, cause and volume status. Bath's primary-care guidance recommends admission below **120**, careful assessment at **120–125**, and an expedient repeat in new cases to exclude rapid deterioration. Its two-week repeat applies to **only mildly abnormal** sodium. Some stable chronic cases can be managed outside hospital, following a defined plan. [Royal United Hospitals Bath: hyponatraemia in primary care, pages 2–3](https://www.ruh.nhs.uk/pathology/documents/clinical_guidelines/PATH-019_hyponatraemia_in_primary_care.pdf).

### 8. A resolved TIA is not treated with thrombolysis
**Note:** [[tia#* just like in stroke|tia — just like in stroke]], line 22.
The instruction to give alteplase for “TIA/stroke” within 4.5 hours conflates two pathways. **Resolved TIA:** immediate aspirin 300 mg unless contraindicated, plus urgent specialist assessment. **Ongoing suspected stroke:** emergency stroke assessment and consideration of reperfusion against eligibility criteria. Presentation within the time window alone does not justify thrombolysis. [NICE NG128, sections 1.1 and 1.4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng128/chapter/Recommendations).

### 9. TIA with AF: a routine two-week delay to anticoagulation is incorrect
**Notes:** [[stroke#Patients with AF + Ischemic TIA|stroke — Patients with AF + Ischemic TIA]], line 28; [[tia]], line 24.
The stroke table explicitly labels its scenario TIA but delays the DOAC for two weeks. The UK stroke guideline recommends rapid-onset anticoagulation for **TIA or non-disabling ischaemic stroke with AF as soon as intracranial bleeding is excluded**, provided there are no other contraindications. Larger strokes require a separate timing decision. It also recommends immediate high-intensity statin treatment after TIA/ischaemic stroke, rather than the note's blanket 48-hour delay. [National Clinical Guideline for Stroke: acute care](https://www.strokeguideline.org/chapter/acute-care/).

### 10. AF under 48 hours does not automatically remove the need for anticoagulation
**Note:** [[atrial fibrillation#Cardioversion|atrial fibrillation — Cardioversion]], line 46.
“Further anticoagulation is unnecessary” is too absolute. NICE recommends anticoagulation after recent-onset AF when sinus rhythm is not restored within 48 hours, recurrence risk is high, or stroke-risk assessment indicates it. Successful cardioversion does not erase underlying stroke risk. [NICE NG196, recommendation 1.8.9 and stroke-risk recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/NG196/chapter/recommendations).

### 11. Meningitis: rash cannot identify the organism by itself
**Note:** [[meningitis#Diagnosis|meningitis — Diagnosis]], lines 28–39.
The split into “no rash = pneumococcus” and “rash = meningococcus” is unreliable. **Meningococcal disease can occur without a rash.** A rash also does not replace assessment for bacterial meningitis or the appropriate blood/CSF investigations. Avoid using rash presence alone as the diagnostic branch. [NICE NG240, recommendation 1.1.10 and diagnostic recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng240/chapter/recommendations).

### 12. Pre-hospital antibiotics: distinguish bacterial meningitis from meningococcal disease
**Notes:** [[meningitis#Suspected meningococcal disease in GP clinic|meningitis — GP treatment]], line 54; [[benzylpenicillin#Suspected Meningitis in GP setting %%embedded%%|benzylpenicillin — suspected meningitis]].
Do not delay emergency transfer to give antibiotics. For **strongly suspected bacterial meningitis**, NICE recommends pre-hospital antibiotics if transfer is likely to be significantly delayed. For **strongly suspected meningococcal disease**, give ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin as soon as possible unless this delays transfer, subject to severe-allergy precautions. The blanket instruction for every suspected meningitis presentation loses this distinction. [NICE NG240, recommendations 1.2.3–1.2.6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng240/chapter/recommendations).

### 13. Pre-eclampsia: birth before 34 weeks may be necessary
**Note:** [[pre-eclampsia#BP|pre-eclampsia — BP]], line 48.
“Don't offer delivery if <34 weeks” is incorrect as an absolute rule. Continue surveillance before 34 weeks **unless there are indications for planned early birth**, such as uncontrolled BP, worsening organ dysfunction, persistent neurological features/eclampsia, abruption or fetal compromise. [NICE NG133, recommendation 1.5.7 and Table 3](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng133/chapter/Recommendations).

### 14. Ramipril monitoring: three weeks is later than standard advice
**Note:** [[hypertension#How to manage a patient in GP with hypertension|hypertension — GP management]], line 45.
After starting or increasing an ACE inhibitor/ARB for hypertension, check **creatinine/eGFR, potassium and sodium within 1–2 weeks**, or within **7 days** with relevant risk factors. The note delays this to three weeks and focuses only on eGFR. [NHS SPS: ACE inhibitor and ARB monitoring](https://sps.nhs.uk/monitorings/ace-inhibitors-and-angiotensin-ii-receptor-blockers-monitoring/).

### 15. Lithium toxicity: bicarbonate is not a standard lithium-removal treatment
**Notes:** [[lithium#Management of Toxicity|lithium — Management of Toxicity]], line 48; [[toxins]], lithium row, line 22.
Remove the instruction to use sodium bicarbonate to promote lithium elimination. The EXTRIP pathway centres on stopping lithium, supportive care, isotonic saline for volume depletion, and extracorporeal removal when indicated. Urinary alkalinisation is not a recommended lithium-elimination strategy in that pathway. [EXTRIP systematic review and recommendations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4422246/), [EXTRIP lithium recommendations](https://www.extrip-workgroup.org/lithium).

## Important exam and guideline corrections

### 16. Clinic BP: record the lower of the last two readings
**Note:** [[hypertension#How to manage a patient in GP with hypertension|hypertension — GP management]], lines 35–36.
“Code the highest reading” conflicts with NICE. Repeat an elevated clinic measurement, take a third if the second differs substantially, and record the **lower of the last two**. The threshold for repeating an inter-arm difference is **>15 mmHg**, not >20; use the higher-reading arm if the difference persists. [NICE NG136, recommendations 1.2.1–1.2.2](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng136/chapter/recommendations).

### 17. Type 2 diabetes still determines step-1 hypertension treatment when ACR is normal
**Note:** [[hypertension#Question|hypertension — Question]], line 78.
The original ARB answer is appropriate, but the added explanation about **ACR <3 → amlodipine first-line because diabetes is managed** is incorrect. Type 2 diabetes remains an indication for an **ACE inhibitor or ARB at step 1**, irrespective of age; prefer an ARB for Black African/African-Caribbean family origin. Normal ACR does not remove that branch. [NICE NG136 treatment visual summary](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng136/resources/visual-summary-pdf-6899919517).

### 18. Carotid endarterectomy: distinguish measurement methods, not men versus women
**Note:** [[tia#Treatment|tia — Treatment]], lines 15–17.
The ≥50% threshold for men and ≥70% for women is not NICE's rule. For appropriate symptomatic patients, NICE uses **50–99% NASCET** or **70–99% ECST** stenosis, with urgent assessment for surgery. These percentages reflect different measurement methods. [NICE NG128, recommendations 1.2.4–1.2.6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng128/chapter/Recommendations).

### 19. Gonorrhoea: ciprofloxacin is not an interchangeable empirical first-line option
**Notes:** [[gonorrhoea#Treatment|gonorrhoea — Treatment]], lines 12–16; [[cervicitis#Treatment|cervicitis — Treatment]], lines 31–32.
Ceftriaxone 1 g IM is the recommended regimen. Ciprofloxacin requires demonstrated susceptibility and clinical justification; BASHH 2025 no longer recommends it as first-line even when susceptibility is known. Also, **routine test of cure is no longer required for every patient**: BASHH specifies persistent symptoms, pharyngeal infection, unknown susceptibility, non-ceftriaxone treatment, or pregnancy. [BASHH 2025 full gonorrhoea guideline](https://www.bashh.org/_userfiles/pages/files/gc_guideline_2025_final.pdf), [BASHH guideline summary](https://www.bashh.org/resources/136/gonorrhoea_2025_updated_guideline/).

### 20. Absence epilepsy and valproate: update the treatment distinction and restrictions
**Note:** [[seizure#Typical (petit mal) Absence seizures|seizure — Absence seizures]], line 51; [[seizure#Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (Janz syndrome)|juvenile myoclonic epilepsy section]].
For **absence seizures alone**, NICE recommends **ethosuximide first-line**; valproate is among second-line options. Absences with other seizure types require a different approach. Add the MHRA restriction: starting valproate in a new patient **under 55, male or female**, requires two specialists to independently document that alternatives are ineffective/not tolerated or that compelling reasons mean reproductive risks do not apply. The JME treatment shortcut needs that qualification too. [NICE NG217, section 5.3](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng217/chapter/treating-epileptic-seizures-in-children-young-people-and-adults), [MHRA February 2025 clarification](https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/valproate-belvo-convulex-depakote-dyzantil-epilim-epilim-chrono-or-chronosphere-episenta-epival-and-syonellv-review-by-two-specialists-is-required-for-initiating-valproate-but-not-for-male-patients-already-taking-valproate).

### 21. Bariatric surgery: “only IUCD” is too restrictive
**Note:** [[contraceptives in emergency#Emergency Contraceptives|contraceptives in emergency — Emergency Contraceptives]], line 16.
FSRH advises favouring **non-oral methods** because oral contraception, including oral EC, may be less effective after bariatric surgery. That does not restrict ongoing contraception to an IUCD: implants, LNG-IUS and injectables are options depending on individual eligibility. For emergency contraception, the copper IUD is preferred; if unacceptable/inappropriate, seek advice about oral options rather than declaring a universal lifetime ban. [FSRH overweight, obesity and contraception guideline, sections 6.2.3.2–6.2.3.3](https://www.cosrh.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/documents/1overweight-obesity-and-contraception-guideline-april-2019.pdf).

### 23. Prematurity example: 28 + 8 = 36 weeks postmenstrual age
**Note:** [[immunisation schedule (paeds)#Special Cases|immunisation schedule (paeds) — Special Cases]], line 18.
A baby born at **28 weeks**, now **8 weeks after birth**, is approximately **36 weeks postmenstrual age**, not still 28 weeks. Your underlying rule to vaccinate by chronological age is correct; this is an arithmetic error in the example.

### 24. Statistical power does not mean overall accuracy
**Note:** [[statistics- errors, confidence intervals#Type 2 error (β)|statistics — Type 2 error and power]], line 43.
The first power bullet defines it as correct identification regardless of accepting/rejecting H0. Power is specifically **P(reject H0 | H0 is false)** for a specified effect and design, equal to **1−β**. The next bullet already gives this correct definition; remove the conflicting first one. The isolated 31% α and 26% β figures also need an explicitly labelled example, since they are not general constants. [Oxford CEBM: statistical approaches to uncertainty, decision table](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/files/ebm-tools/statisticalapproachestouncertainty.pdf).
