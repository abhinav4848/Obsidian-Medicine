## Exam and preparation
Twelve simulated remote consultations, each 12 minutes with three minutes' reading time. Cases involve patients, carers or professionals by video or audio. See [[SCA mock exam framework]] for the domains: data gathering/diagnosis, clinical management/medical complexity, relating to others. Examination performance is assessed elsewhere, but interpreting supplied findings and arranging examination remain important.

Read for identity, setting, task, prior events, medicines, results and gaps. Decide where you join the patient's journey. Prepare urgent decisions without locking into a diagnosis; reset after each case.
## Open and focus
Let the opening statement finish; pause for extra information. Use a focused open invitation, then targeted questions. New unexplained symptoms need history from onset; reviews need current change, control and patient agenda rather than a complete retelling.

Use relevant SOCRATES elements for pain: site, onset, character, radiation, associations, timing, aggravating/relieving factors, severity/function. For other symptoms establish onset, duration, progression, triggers, relief and associations. Use records actively: “I see you started this last week; how have you been getting on?”
## Three areas to explore
### Safety
Identify action needed now, today or urgently. Signpost specific warning questions, avoid leading negatives and follow positive findings promptly. Include safeguarding, driving, machinery, medicine risk and dependants where relevant. Mnemonics do not replace judgement.
### Life and practical context
Explore how life affects illness **and** illness affects life. Work, alcohol, smoking and home are prompts adapted to relevance; add sleep, stress, diet, activity, travel, recreational drugs and sexual context as indicated.
- Home: actual support, ADLs and caring duties, not just who lives there.
- Work: duties, hazards, impact, employment status and affordability of time off.
- Habits: amount, reasons, previous attempts and readiness for support.
Follow through answers: “How has that affected work?”
### Understanding, concerns and goals
Ask “What have you been thinking?” “What worries you most?” “What were you hoping we could achieve?” Allow concerns about work or family as well as diagnosis. Reflect important answers. If a concern is already stated, acknowledge it and ask whether anything else matters. Do not repeat questions as if you have not listened.
## Relevant background
Use PMH, drugs, allergies, family history and systems review selectively. When important, ask properly: actual drug use, OTC/herbal/injected treatments, contraception, adverse effects and what an alleged allergy involved. Explore disease-specific family history; avoid a routine tour of every organ system.
## Diagnosis and explanation
State the working conclusion and reasoning. If uncertain, explain the leading possibilities and how you will clarify them. Ask what the patient knows and whether an explanation would help. Explain in two or three plain sentences, then check understanding. Give results promptly enough to meet the patient's needs; do not delay by a fixed formula.
## Management
1. Give the headline: diagnosis or working differential.
2. Tailor explanation to existing understanding.
3. Bring together clinical needs, worries, function and practical barriers.
4. Lead when safety requires it; otherwise agree the priority.
5. Discuss realistic choices, benefits/harms and preferences; agree who does what and when.

For example: “We need to treat the attacks, look at the work trigger and discuss your scan concern. First I need to check you today; then we can work through longer-term options.”

Destinations: emergency transfer, same-day assessment/admission, urgent referral, routine referral, planned examination/review or remote care. Listing issues does not substitute for addressing them.
## Relating to others
**Reasoning:** explain unexpected questions. **Relevance:** choose questions for this person. **Reflection:** respond to answers and emotion rather than saying “okay” and moving on.

Acknowledge cues now and return appropriately: “Work sounds important; I'll come back after checking these symptoms.” Actually return. Use silence thoughtfully; describe emotion tentatively without claiming to know exactly how someone feels. Chunk information and invite questions or teach-back.
## Closing and recovery when stuck
Agree actions, timing, responsibility and what to do if the plan fails. Where deterioration is possible, explain specific warning symptoms, urgency and contact route. A planned safety-net must actually be delivered. Arrange follow-up and check understanding.

If blank: pause, check safety, life context and the main concern; identify the next action. A short summary can restore direction. Arrange concrete follow-up for unfinished issues after checking they can safely wait.
## Example consultation phrases
### Opening, questions and relevant background
- **Locate pain:** “Do you mind pointing to where the pain is?” (V2, 09:57)
- **Signpost safety questions:** “When it comes to indigestion, there are a couple of medical questions—yes/no questions—that we tend to ask. I'm just going to run through a few.” (V2, 18:23)
- **Use the record:** “Looking at my notes, I can see you're taking aspirin, an ACE inhibitor and propranolol. Is that right?” (V2, 24:53)
- **Ask specifically about contraception:** “Can I check whether you take any contraceptive tablets or pills?” (V2, 26:36)
- **Explain an allergy question:** “We do prescribe medication for this. Can I check whether you have any allergies that you know of?” (V2, 27:45)
- **Clarify an allergy:** “Can I ask what happens when you have penicillin? Where did that allergy label come from?” (V2, 28:09)
- **Relevant family history:** “Sometimes heart problems can run in families. Is there anyone in your family who's had heart disease or heart problems?” (V2, 28:58)
- **Breast family history:** “Has anyone else in your family had problems with their breasts—conditions, diagnoses or treatment?” (V2, 29:11)
- **Link occupation to symptoms:** “Certain environments can trigger coughs, such as cold or dusty environments. Can I check what you do for a job?” (V3, 04:02)
- **Link habits to coping:** “Sometimes when people have pain for a long time, they turn to other things they think might help, such as smoking or alcohol. Is that something you do?” (V3, 05:55)
- **Introduce life context:** “When it comes to headaches, there are often links with our lives. Do you mind if I ask a few things about you and your lifestyle?” (V3, 07:35)
### Understanding, worries and reflection
- **Broad ideas:** “What were your thoughts about these symptoms? Was anything going through your mind about all of this?” (V3, 09:29)
- **Broad concerns:** “Was there anything worrying you about all of this?” (V3, 11:43)
- **Already disclosed concern:** “I know you're really worried about cancer, and we're going to talk about that in detail. Was there anything else particularly worrying you?” (V3, 16:01)
- **Reflect the answer:** “I can see why you think that.” / “I can see where that comes from. That makes sense now.” Then explore the worry or request that follows. (V3, 18:27–19:23)
- **Reflect a visible cue:** “I can see you're rubbing your ear a few times. Is everything okay?” (V5, 07:28)
- **Allow space:** “I can see this is difficult to talk about. Take your time.” (V5, 07:23)
- **Perspective-taking:** “If I'd been sitting waiting for this call while I was busy myself, I'd feel frustrated too.” Use only where it fits the person's account. (V5, 06:10)
### Results, diagnosis and uncertainty
- **Acknowledge known findings:** “I noticed your blood pressure and heart rate yesterday were fine, but your BMI was higher than a few years ago.” (V3, 25:44)
- **Acknowledge historical results:** “I noticed your liver blood tests were normal a year ago. It would be useful to see what they show now.” (V3, 27:43)
- **Results agenda:** “I have your results here and we can go through them. Do you mind if I ask a couple of questions first, to understand the story and make sure we're on the right track?” (V3, 29:28)
- **Flex when distressed:** “I can see you're really keen to get these results and you're worried. Let's talk about the results now.” (V3, 30:10)
- **Clear working diagnosis:** “Putting everything together and listening to your story, I think you have depression.” Use when supported by the assessment. (V3, 31:24)
- **Honest uncertainty:** “I'll be honest: I'm not completely sure what's going on yet, but a few things are going through my mind…” Name the relevant possibilities and next steps. (V2, 03:30; V3, 31:53)
### Explanation and shared management
- **Offer explanation:** “It sounds like you have something called a migraine. Is that something you've heard of? Would you like me to explain it?” (V4, 04:05)
- **Check and pause:** “Does that make sense? Are you happy with that?” Allow an answer before adding more. (V4, 05:07)
- **Provisional conclusion:** “Listening to the pattern you're describing, it sounds as though it might be a chest infection, but we need to do a few things before we can clarify that.” (V4, 11:09)
- **Explain examination:** “I'd like to arrange for you to come in later today so I can examine you. I'll explain how we'll arrange that.” (V4, 11:33)
- **Put issues on the table:** “There are a few important things we need to talk about: treating the migraines, the stress at work, possible triggers and your question about prevention.” (V4, 28:13)
- **Acknowledge an option not yet indicated:** “Preventative treatment may not be the next step at this stage, but I know it's on your mind, and we can talk about when we would consider it.” (V4, 09:14; 28:40)
- **Clinical priority:** “Out of all those things, I think first we need to focus on getting you assessed today.” (V4, 19:26)
- **Patient priority:** “There are a few different things going on. Is there one you'd like to focus on first?” (V4, 20:14; V5, 11:31)
- **Introduce symptom treatment:** “One thing we can talk about is how to treat your pain [or nausea/arthritis/migraine].” (V4, 14:01)
- **Introduce safety-netting as an issue:** “Before we end the call, I want to go through symptoms that would mean you need to contact us sooner or go to hospital.” Give the actual symptoms and action, not just this introduction. (V4, 31:29)
- **Focused opening:** “You mentioned the pain—could you tell me more about that?” (V2, 07:25)
- **Prompt relevant radiation:** “Does it spread to your left arm or jaw?” Ask after the open description when clinically relevant. (V2, 11:14)
- **Use existing support:** “You mentioned your wife is worried about this [or already helps you with this]…” Bring their actual account into the plan. (V3, 03:27)
- **Already stated expectation:** “I know you mentioned wanting a CT scan, and we'll discuss that. Was there anything else you thought we could do while you're here?” (V3, 16:16)
- **Planned review:** “We may not get through all of this today, so let's make a start and arrange to catch up in a couple of weeks. Was there one thing you wanted to start with?” Tailor the review interval to clinical need. (V4, 28:48)
