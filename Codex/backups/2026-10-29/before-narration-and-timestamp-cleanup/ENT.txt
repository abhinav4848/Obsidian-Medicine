## Shared ENT assessment
Establish onset, duration, laterality, progression, pain, discharge/bleeding, hearing, tinnitus, vertigo and systemic illness. Review trauma, swimming/diving/flying, foreign bodies, surgery, recurrent disease and ototoxic medicines. Ask about work/noise exposure, communication, sleep, mood and function. Examination should include relevant ENT, nodes and neurological findings; remote diagnosis has limits.
## Ear pain and infection
- **Otitis externa:** canal inflammation, pain moving tragus/pinna, itch/discharge; assess eczema, water and instrumentation. Keep dry, avoid cotton buds, offer analgesia and suitable topical treatment. Check drum integrity before potentially ototoxic drops. Persistent/recurrent disease may need swab, aural toilet or ENT.
- **Necrotising otitis externa:** severe persistent pain, discharge, diabetes/immunosuppression or cranial neuropathy → urgent same-day ENT/hospital assessment. “Malignant” here describes aggressive infection, not cancer.
- **Acute otitis media:** acute pain ± fever, bulging drum/effusion, sometimes perforation. Explain usual course of about three days, sometimes a week, and provide analgesia. Many children need no antibiotic or a back-up prescription. Otorrhoea or bilateral disease under two may increase benefit but does not automatically mandate antibiotics; systemic illness/high complication risk does. Amoxicillin is usual first-line when indicated, with age/allergy-appropriate alternatives. Mastoid swelling/tenderness, protruding ear or severe illness needs hospital assessment. [NICE NG91](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng91/chapter/Recommendations)
- **OME/glue ear:** fluid without acute infection, causing hearing/developmental or school problems. Assess hearing and impact; observation, hearing support and selected grommet/adenoid options follow the relevant pathway. Adult unilateral effusion needs assessment for an underlying cause.
## Tinnitus
Characterise the sound, side, pulsatility/heartbeat relation, onset, hearing loss and neurological symptoms. Review noise, medicines, jaw/ear disease and impact on sleep/mood. Ask directly about safety if severe distress suggests self-harm risk.

Sudden neurological signs or acute uncontrolled vertigo require immediate assessment. Sudden hearing loss uses the urgent pathway below. Persistent unilateral or pulsatile tinnitus needs investigation/referral rather than reassurance alone. Offer audiological assessment; manage hearing loss and distress, including appropriate psychological support. Explain sound enrichment as an optional coping strategy rather than a guaranteed cure.
## Hearing loss
Distinguish sudden/rapid onset from longstanding decline; assess communication needs and adapt the consultation (clear speech, written information, hearing aids/interpreter where appropriate). Examine for wax, infection and drum disease. Tuning forks help distinguish conductive/sensorineural patterns but do not replace audiometry.

**Rinne:** air > bone is normal or sensorineural loss; bone ≥ air suggests conductive loss. **Weber:** lateralises to affected side in conductive loss and better side in sensorineural loss, interpreted with the overall examination.

Unexplained sudden loss over ≤3 days occurring within 30 days → ENT/ED assessment within 24 hours. Sudden onset >30 days ago or rapid deterioration over 4–90 days → urgent assessment within two weeks. Facial droop/sensory change or possible stroke demands immediate appropriate pathway. [NICE NG98](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng98/chapter/recommendations)
## Cholesteatoma and vestibular schwannoma
**Cholesteatoma:** recurrent offensive discharge, conductive loss and abnormal/retracted drum or debris; keratin accumulation can erode structures. Refer ENT; facial weakness, severe vertigo or intracranial/septic signs increases urgency. Repeated antibiotics alone do not solve it.

**Vestibular schwannoma:** consider with unilateral/asymmetric sensorineural loss, tinnitus or imbalance; specialist assessment/MRI may be required. Explain that monitoring, surgery or radiotherapy depends on size, symptoms and individual risk. Do not equate a benign diagnosis with no possible effect.
## Dizziness and vertigo
Clarify spinning, presyncope, imbalance or nonspecific light-headedness. Ask timing/triggers, hearing symptoms, headache, focal neurology, cardiac symptoms, medicines, falls and hydration. Assess stroke/central causes and cardiac disease before assuming an ear cause. Acute continuous vertigo with new neurological signs or severe gait impairment requires urgent stroke assessment. HINTS is for appropriately trained clinicians in the correct acute vestibular syndrome, not a general remote screening test.

| Pattern | Likely approach |
|---|---|
| Brief position-triggered vertigo without hearing loss | BPPV: appropriate Dix–Hallpike and repositioning/Epley after safety checks |
| Continuous vertigo after viral illness, no hearing loss | Vestibular neuritis after excluding central causes; short symptomatic treatment then mobilisation/rehabilitation |
| Vertigo plus acute hearing loss | Do not casually label labyrinthitis; urgent hearing-loss assessment |
| Episodic vertigo, fluctuating hearing, tinnitus/fullness | Consider Ménière disease; ENT/audiovestibular assessment |
Avoid prolonged vestibular suppressants. Discuss driving, heights, machinery, falls and home support. Brandt–Daroff exercises may suit selected BPPV cases with instruction.
## Sore throat and tonsillitis
Assess duration, fever, cough/coryza, swallowing, hydration, voice, nodes, immunosuppression and medicines such as carbimazole. Ask about unilateral pain/trismus, drooling, stridor and severe systemic illness.

- Airway compromise, suspected epiglottitis, deep-neck infection or severe sepsis → emergency care. Keep a distressed child calm; do not force throat examination in suspected epiglottitis.
- Quinsy: unilateral severe pain, muffled voice, trismus/uvular displacement → same-day ENT/hospital assessment.
- Uncomplicated sore throat usually improves within a week. Analgesia and fluids; FeverPAIN/Centor support antibiotic decisions. Phenoxymethylpenicillin is usual first-line when indicated; choose current age/allergy-appropriate regimen.
- Recurrent disabling, documented tonsillitis: referral commonly uses ≥7 episodes/year, ≥5/year for two years or ≥3/year for three years, with clinical judgement and impact. Tonsillectomy does not prevent every future sore throat.
## Glandular fever
Consider EBV with sore throat, marked fatigue and cervical nodes ± hepatosplenomegaly. Tests depend on age/timing and differential; avoid reflex amoxicillin. Explain self-limiting infection but potentially prolonged fatigue. Assess airway/hydration. Avoid contact sports/heavy exertion during splenic-risk period and individualise return advice. Severe abdominal pain/collapse or breathing difficulty needs urgent help.
## Hoarseness, vocal lesions and stridor
Acute laryngitis often follows infection/voice strain; advise hydration and voice care, avoiding smoke. Persistent unexplained hoarseness, neck mass, haemoptysis or swallowing symptoms needs cancer assessment, particularly with smoking/alcohol risk. Vocal nodules/palsy require specialist evaluation and possible voice therapy.

Stridor is an airway sign, not a diagnosis. Assess distress, drooling, foreign body, anaphylaxis, croup and epiglottitis. Severe obstruction needs emergency help; do not delay while pursuing a routine ENT history. See [[Paediatrics]].
## Epistaxis and nasal injury
For an active uncomplicated nosebleed: sit forward, pinch the **soft part** continuously for 10–15 minutes, breathe through the mouth and spit out blood. Heavy bleeding, instability, breathing difficulty, head injury or bleeding persisting beyond this needs urgent assessment. Review anticoagulants, other bleeding, trauma, nasal drugs and recurrence; do not stop prescribed anticoagulation casually. After control, avoid picking/blowing, heavy exertion, alcohol/hot drinks for the initial period. [NHS nosebleed advice](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/nosebleed/)

After nasal trauma assess other facial/head injuries, obstruction and **septal haematoma** (immediate ENT/ED). Deformity may need timely ENT review once swelling allows assessment; do not miss the window for reduction.
## Polyps, rhinitis and sinusitis
- **Nasal polyps:** obstruction, smell loss, asthma/NSAID sensitivity context. Unilateral mass, bleeding or atypical symptoms needs ENT assessment. Intranasal steroids and technique/adherence are key in appropriate bilateral inflammatory disease.
- **Allergic rhinitis:** seasonal/perennial trigger pattern, sneezing, itch, watery discharge and impact on sleep/asthma. Allergen measures, non-sedating antihistamine and/or intranasal steroid according to symptoms; demonstrate technique and avoid decongestant overuse.
- **Acute sinusitis:** assess duration, unilateral severe pain, fever, purulent discharge and deterioration after initial improvement. Most early cases are viral; analgesia and advice are often sufficient. Antibiotic choice follows severity, risk and current guidance. Orbital swelling, visual change, severe frontal headache or neurological signs → hospital urgently.
## Head and neck cancer prompts
Persistent unexplained neck lump; persistent unexplained hoarseness in the relevant age group; oral ulcer lasting >3 weeks; concerning oral patches/lumps, unilateral obstruction/bleeding or unexplained otalgia with normal ear examination. Explain suspected-cancer referral and follow-up rather than promising a universal appointment date.
## Example consultation phrases
- **Ear pain:** “Where exactly do you feel the pain—on the outside of the ear or further into the canal?” (V23, 00:55)
- **Otitis externa:** “This means inflammation or infection of the outer part of the ear, including the ear canal where you're feeling the pain.” (V23, 07:34)
- **Otitis media:** “It's inflammation or infection in the middle part of the ear, behind the eardrum. It can be caused by a virus or by bacteria.” (V23, 13:03)
- **Glue ear:** “There's fluid collecting in the middle ear, but it isn't the same as an acute ear infection.” (V23, 17:52)
- **Grommets:** “These small tubes help ventilate the middle ear. They usually come out by themselves; the ENT team will explain the follow-up.” (V23, 20:49)
- **Simple hearing test:** “I'll stand behind you, mask the sound in one ear and whisper into the other.” (V24, 07:31)
- **Rinne test:** “I'll place this vibrating tuning fork behind the ear, then in front of it. Please tell me when and where you hear the sound.” (V24, 08:08)
- **Weber test:** “I'll put the vibrating tuning fork in the middle of your forehead. Do you hear it more loudly on one side?” (V24, 09:26)
- **Audiometry:** “You'll wear headphones and hear sounds at different pitches and volumes. You press a button whenever you hear one, and the results are plotted on a graph.” (V24, 11:10)
- **Dizziness:** “People can mean different things by dizziness. Could you explain what it feels like for you? Is there a spinning sensation?” (V24, 18:45)
- **BPPV by breaking down the name:** “Positional means it happens when you move your head; paroxysmal means it comes and goes; vertigo means the spinning sensation. Benign means this particular condition isn't caused by a dangerous process.” Use after appropriate assessment, without dismissing disabling symptoms. (V24, 27:38)
- **Position triggers:** “Does it happen when you roll over in bed, look up or turn your head?” (V24, 28:24)
- **Dix–Hallpike:** “We'll turn your head and help you lie back while watching your eyes. This can bring on the spinning briefly and help us identify the cause.” Check suitability first. (V24, 28:54)
- **Epley:** “A sequence of head movements can help reposition particles in the inner ear.” (V24, 29:58)
- **Nosebleed first aid:** “Sit upright and lean forward with your mouth open. Pinch the soft part of the nose continuously for 10–15 minutes.” Follow with the escalation advice in the clinical section. (V25, 25:42)
- **Persistent nosebleed:** “The hospital team may seal the bleeding point or use a pack to apply pressure if it won't stop.” (V25, 26:06)
- **Rhinitis triggers:** “Can I ask about things that make it worse—for example, being around particular grasses or trees?” (V25, 31:28)
