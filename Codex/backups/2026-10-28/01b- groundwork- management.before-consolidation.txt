# Groundwork — management
**Video:** 4. Groundwork- Management
**Core principle: situation management before condition management.** Use the clinical findings, the patient's life and their ICE to build one plan. Treating the diagnosis is only part of managing the consultation.

## The three management steps
### Step 1 — diagnosis or key differentials
- Can I make a working diagnosis from the information available?
- If not, what are the key differentials?
- Have I explained my reasoning and the uncertainty clearly?
- Do I sound confident about the **next step**, even when the diagnosis is uncertain?

**When the diagnosis is supported:**
> “Putting everything together and listening to your story, I think you have depression.”

**When it remains uncertain:**
> “I'll be honest: I'm not completely sure what's going on yet, but a few possibilities are going through my mind…”

Name the relevant possibilities and explain how you will clarify them. Do not invent certainty to sound confident.
### Step 2 — offer an explanation
Find out what they already know and whether an explanation would help. Start with two or three plain-language sentences, then pause rather than giving a long lecture.
> “It sounds like you've got something called a migraine. Is that something you've heard of? Would you like me to explain it in more detail?”

If they understand already, move on. If they want more information, explain max 2-3 lines and check:
> “Does that make sense? Is there a particular part you'd like me to go through again?”

Only explain if asked as not much marks in it. Also keep it 2-3 lines and max 60-90secs. 
### Step 3 — put the issues on the table
✅**Name the main things that need addressing before going deeply into one of them.** This shows how the history has shaped your plan and helps agree priorities.

Don't start off with treatment options first. (We can do this for your migraine, that for your migraine ❌), cuz then you'll get stuck in that conversation regardless of other issues. 

An **issue** is what needs addressing; an **option** is one way of addressing it. For migraine, the issues might be treating attacks, work stress, an apparent trigger and a request for prevention. Different medicines are options within the first issue.

There are a few things here that we need to have a chat about.

Bring back **their actual issues**. Do not recite all five categories in every consultation.

Useful linking phrases:
- “There are a few important things I think we need to talk about.”
- “You mentioned things have been difficult with your relationship. There may be ways we can help.”
- “I know cancer is on your mind, and we need to talk about that concern.”
- “You mentioned smoking, and there is support available if you'd like to discuss it.”
- “Driving is another important thing we need to talk about.”

Pause after outlining the agenda. Naming an issue starts the discussion; essential treatment, referral and safety instructions still need to be completed.
## Who leads next?
After the three steps, decide what needs attention first. Both approaches should involve the patient and respond to their concerns.
### Invite the patient's priority when there is no urgent clinical priority
> “There are a few different things going on. Obviously we're going to get you in at some point to do a proper examination, and make sure things are okay from that point of view. But was there anything particular out of those issues that you wanted to focus on first?”

For a stable migraine review, the patient's main priority might be treatment, work stress or understanding prevention. Do not assume the medicine discussion is always the part they most need.
### Take the lead when clinical urgency determines the order
> “Out of all those things, I think first we need to get you assessed today. I'd like to examine you and decide what treatment you need.”

Explain **why** this comes first, explore barriers and agree the practical arrangements. Do not let a lower-priority discussion delay necessary assessment or treatment.
## Worked example — migraine
**Information gathered:** recurrent migraine; increasing stress at work; wine seems to trigger attacks; the patient wants to discuss preventative medicine. The assessment has not identified an acute concern.
### Diagnosis and offer of explanation
> “From the pattern you've described, this sounds like migraine. Is that something you know about, or would it help if I explained it briefly?”
### Put all four issues on the table
> “There are a few things I think we should talk about. 
> - Firstly, how to treat the migraines when they happen. 
> - Secondly, you mentioned that work has become stressful, and the headaches seem to have become more frequent around the same time. 
> - Thirdly, you've noticed a link with drinking wine, so we could look at that trigger. 
> - And fourthly, I know you wanted to discuss treatment to prevent the attacks.”
### Address the request without deciding too early
> “Preventative treatment is something we can discuss. Whether it's the best next step depends on how often the attacks happen, their impact and the treatments you've already tried.”

The prevention request deserves discussion even if your assessment leads to recommending a different next step.
### Agree priority and review
> “Was there one of those things you'd like to start with?”

If a further review is needed -> Get the part about you following up with her early in your discussion as well. Even after the issues are all laid out. 
> “We may not get through all of this today, so let's make a start and arrange to catch up to see how you're getting on.”

Choose the review interval according to the clinical situation.

**Then develop the chosen issue:** discuss suitable options, benefits and harms, preference and a practical next step. Return to the other priorities as time permits. A long conversation about one medicine should not obscure the work issue, trigger or prevention request.
## Worked example — suspected chest infection
**Information gathered:** ongoing cough; possible chest infection; smoking; travel planned in a month; a request for an X-ray. In this worked scenario, examination is needed today and the initial assessment has not identified an emergency.
### Provisional diagnosis and offer of explanation
**Provisional diagnosis:**
> “Mister x, now this could be a number of different things, but listening to the story, listening to the pattern of what you're describing, it sounds like it might be a chest infection, but again we need to do a few things before we can clarify that.”

**Check familiarity with chest infection, then offer an explanation:**
> “Is a chest infection something you've heard of before? Do you want me to explain that in more detail?”
### Put the issues on the table
> “I'm really glad you got on this call today because there are a couple of important things that we need to talk about. Firstly, I think we do need to assess you. We do need to examine you just to make sure this is in fact what we're dealing with.
>
> So I'd like to arrange you to come in later today, and I'll talk about how we can do that and we'll get that arranged. Secondly, if it is a chest infection, I think we need to think about treatment. Potentially, some antibiotics might be necessary here. Again, we'll know more once we've examined you and seen you in the practice. Thirdly, you mentioned that you smoke.
>
> When you come, I think it's important that we talk about that because smoking can lead to these things getting worse, and there's lots of support available if you'd like to talk about that. And finally, I know you're worried about going away, weren't you? We need to discuss that in terms of how we look at you over the next couple of weeks to make sure you're okay before you travel in four weeks.”
### Clinician-led priority — assessment today
> “Now, mister X, out of all of those things, I think first and foremost, it's probably important that we get you in today. I'd like to examine you.”
### Safety-netting — make it an issue in itself
**Add safety-netting to the issues on the table:** the patient could deteriorate before the afternoon assessment.
> “And fourthly, even though you're gonna be coming later this afternoon, I wanna go through a couple of symptoms to look out for that mean you need to get in touch much quicker or even go up to the hospital because chest infections can get quite worse and I'd like to go through the symptoms before we end the call.”
## Six possible consultation endpoints
Think about the likely destination while gathering data; be ready to change it when new information appears.

| Endpoint                                              | Examples from the slide                                    | What you need to make clear                                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Emergency admission<br>ie 999                         | • Current chest pain<br>• Meningitis<br>• Acute abdomen    | Immediate help, safe transport, what to do while waiting and support for dependants          |
| Same day admission                                    | • Pyelonephritis<br>• Temporal arteritis with eye change   | Why **today**, the appropriate team, how the patient will get there and what happens next    |
| Urgent clinic referral<br>(including 2-week referral) | • Suspected cancers<br>• Suspected cholesteatoma           | Reason, urgency, expected contact and action if symptoms worsen or no appointment arrives    |
| Routine referral                                      | • Recurrent otitis media<br>• Considering knee replacement | Purpose, likely next steps, interim care and review arrangements                             |
| GP appointment                                        | • Look in ear<br>• Assess rash                             | When and why examination is needed, what will be assessed and what to do meanwhile           |
| Manage on the video call itself                       | • Mild depression<br>• Acne                                | An agreed treatment/self-care plan, understanding and appropriate follow-up or return advice |
The destination is part of the plan, not the whole plan. Explain the reasoning, logistics and care needed while waiting.
## Follow-up and safety-netting
### Plan necessary follow-up early
When review matters, **include it after outlining the issues rather than hoping to squeeze it in at the end.**
> “There are several things to work through. Let's start today and arrange a review to see how you're progressing.”

Make the arrangement specific: **when, with whom, by what method and for what purpose**. Explain who will review results or check the response. A routine extra appointment is not necessary for every problem.
### Tailor the safety net
#### When do I need to safety-net?
**When the presentation could worsen or change before the planned next step, and the patient would need to act sooner.** An afternoon assessment does not remove the need to cover the hours before it.

Safety-netting is not an automatic list of warning symptoms for every case. The transcript contrasts an acute chest infection with longstanding knee OA or erectile dysfunction, where a routine list of acute warning signs may add little. Base it on the actual presentation and plan.
#### Make it an issue in itself
**If you feel it's important, put the safety net as one of the issues.** Introduce it when putting the issues on the table, rather than leaving it until the end and risking running out of time.
> “But if you do feel like you need to safety net somebody, then it's big enough to be an issue in itself. So when you put the issues on the table, bring it in at that point. Again, don't leave it as a risk that I'll do it at the end just before the bell goes, and then the bell goes and you don't do it.”

Then give the specific symptoms and actions during the consultation. Announcing the issue is the start; the patient still needs to hear what to do.
#### Transcript example — chest infection awaiting afternoon assessment
**Video 4, 00:30:57–00:31:42.** The patient will be examined later that afternoon, but could become worse in the meantime. Safety-netting is therefore the **fourth issue**, alongside assessment/treatment, smoking and travel:
> “Now, mister X, there are a few things that I think we need to have a bit of a chat about, a few important things. Firstly, I'd like to actually get you in later today to examine you and just clarify what we're dealing with, and maybe we need to start you on some treatment if things show like it is an infection. Secondly, you mentioned that you're a smoker. I think at some point we should talk about that because there are some links between smoking and chest infections, and certainly while you're unwell, we should think about holding that off.
>
> Thirdly, you mentioned that you're traveling in a month, we need to put a plan in place to make sure that you're okay by then. So maybe we'll look at a review just before you go to make sure things are okay. And fourthly, even though you're gonna be coming later this afternoon, I wanna go through a couple of symptoms to look out for that mean you need to get in touch much quicker or even go up to the hospital because chest infections can get quite worse and I'd like to go through the symptoms before we end the call.”

**Key point:** “Even though you're gonna be coming later this afternoon…” — the plan must cover deterioration **between now and that appointment**.
#### Contact advice for the interval before assessment
- For worsening symptoms needing urgent advice, contact the practice sooner; if the GP cannot be reached or is closed, **call NHS 111** rather than simply waiting for the afternoon appointment. [NHS 111](https://www.londonambulance.nhs.uk/our-services/urgent-care/when-to-use-111/).
- For **severe difficulty breathing or severe central chest pain that is not going away, call 999**. [NHS chest-infection advice](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/infections-and-poisoning/chest-infection/).

Make clear **which change to watch for, how urgently to act and whom to contact**. Check the patient understands. [RCGP clinical management toolkit](https://www.rcgp.org.uk/mrcgp-exams/simulated-consultation-assessment/toolkit-clinical-management).
## Common management questions
### Do I need to finish every issue?
Aim to complete the essential decisions and a safe, agreed next step within the consultation. Do not rush through every possible issue merely to reach a closing line. Prioritise, take action and make a clear plan for anything deferred.

Mentioning several issues without managing the important ones is insufficient. Equally, a detailed drug discussion should not consume the time needed for the necessary referral or safety plan. [RCGP SCA feedback statements](https://www.rcgp.org.uk/mrcgp-exams/simulated-consultation-assessment/feedback-statements).
### How do I improve time management?
Organise rather than speed up: give a clear working conclusion, offer the explanation, outline the priorities and make the first decision. Then discuss the chosen issue in manageable chunks, listening and checking understanding.
### What if I don't know the diagnosis?
Acknowledge uncertainty, explain the leading possibilities and agree how to clarify them. Sound confident about a safe plan; do not force a diagnosis or delay necessary urgent action.
> “I'm not completely sure which of these is causing it yet. The next step is…”
### What if I don't know the exact medicine or dose?
Use an appropriate prescribing reference or seek advice before prescribing. Check the patient's medicines, allergies, contraindications and monitoring requirements. Do not guess.
> “I think this is the appropriate treatment, but before prescribing it I'll check the BNF to confirm the dose and that it's suitable for you.”

Knowing where to confirm a routine detail supports safe prescribing; recognising and acting on an emergency remains essential.
## Final check
- Did I give a working diagnosis or explain the uncertainty?
- Did I bring back the important red-flag, psychosocial and ICE findings?
- Did we decide what comes first and take the necessary action?
- Is the treatment/referral plan safe, suitable and feasible?
- Does the patient know the next step, any review and when to seek help sooner?
- Have I checked understanding and addressed barriers?
