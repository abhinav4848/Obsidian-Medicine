Androgens are a family of steroid hormones. 
Not just "male hormones". 

They traditionally contribute to male-pattern sexual development & effects. 
Women produce androgens too. They are involved in libido, hair growth, bone and muscle physiology, etc.

# Types of Androgens
| Androgen                  | Where mainly produced             | Potency       | Notes                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Testosterone              | Testes, ovaries, adrenal glands   | Strong        |                                                                                                                                               |
| DHT (dihydrotestosterone) | Made from testosterone in tissues | Very strong   |                                                                                                                                               |
| DHEA                      | Adrenal glands                    | Weak          | dehydroepiandrosterone, not circulating much                                                                                                  |
| DHEA-S                    | Adrenal cortex (zona reticularis) | Weak          | Sulphate attachment makes it water soluble, so circulates in blood & has longer t1/2. <br>It's a useful marker of adrenal androgen production |
| Androstenedione           | Adrenals + ovaries/testes         | Weak/moderate |                                                                                                                                               |
### DHEA-S
DHEA-S is weak androgen but it can act as a precursor for stronger androgens.
`DHEA-S (weak) → DHEA → androstenedione → testosterone → DHT (potent)`
### Testosterone -> DHT
```
Testosterone
|
| 5α-reductase
↓ 
DHT
```

DHT is considerably more potent than testosterone at the androgen receptor.

This is particularly important for things such as:
- Facial/body hair
- Male-pattern hair growth
- Prostate development
- External genital development

So when you think **androgenic effects**, don't just think testosterone.
### DHEA-S vs Testosterone
Suppose a woman develops rapidly progressive [[hirsutism]]/virilisation.
You measure: `Total testosterone ↑↑↑`

This could potentially be:
- ovarian tumour
- ovarian hyperandrogenism
- adrenal source, etc.

So you can look at DHEA-S.
- DHEA-S markedly elevated → suggests adrenal androgen excess
- Testosterone markedly elevated + DHEA-S relatively normal → suggests ovarian source more strongly.

It's not an absolute rule, but it's a useful localisation clue.
# Serum Total vs Free Testosterone

> Total testosterone = all testosterone in the blood  
> Free testosterone = the small fraction that is biologically available to tissues
### Why can total T be normal but free T be high?
Most testosterone in the blood is bound to proteins, mainly:
- **SHBG** (sex hormone-binding globulin): Binds testosterone strongly
- **Albumin**: binds it more loosely
- A tiny amount is **free**. 

So, **Total testosterone = bound + free testosterone**

A woman can therefore have a **normal total testosterone but disproportionately high free testosterone** if her SHBG is low.

For example: 

| |Patient A|Patient B|
|---|--:|--:|
|Total testosterone|1.5|1.5|
|SHBG|Normal|**Low**|
|Free testosterone|Normal|**↑**|
# Free androgen Index %%linked%%
SHBG concentration is increased by the COCP, and reduced by insulin resistance and obesity. 
Free androgen index (FAI) may be a better measure in these situations because it indicates the testosterone which can exert its effect on tissues:
- `FAI = (total testosterone/SHBG) x 100%`
No COCP for 3 months prior to testing (Switch to non-hormonal contraception). 
In normal woman, FAI should be less than 3%.
