## Shared assessment: alcohol or drug use
Use a neutral opening: “Can you talk me through what you use in a typical day?” Establish substance, strength, quantity, route, frequency, last use, duration, recent changes and use with alcohol/sedatives or other drugs. Include prescribed opioids and medicines obtained elsewhere.

Ask about craving, tolerance, loss of control, withdrawal, unsuccessful attempts to stop and harms. Find out **why help is being sought today**, what the substance does for the person and what change they want. Explore previous treatment and what helped or failed.

Assess physical complications, mental state, suicidal thoughts, psychosis, cognition and pregnancy. Ask about housing, money, employment, driving, relationships, domestic abuse, caring for children and other safeguarding concerns. Check available support without assuming relatives are safe to involve.

**Immediate priorities:** reduced consciousness/respiratory depression, severe withdrawal, seizure, delirium, suicidal crisis, psychosis with danger, sepsis/injecting complications or serious medical illness. Arrange emergency or same-day care according to severity before discussing a long-term plan.
## Alcohol quantity and screening
**Units = volume in mL × ABV (%) ÷ 1,000.** Ask about actual serving sizes, including home measures.

| Drink | Example units |
|---|---:|
| 500 mL beer at 5% | 2.5 |
| 175 mL wine at 12% | 2.1 |
| 750 mL wine at 13% | 9.75 |
| 25 mL spirit at 40% | 1 |
UK lower-risk guidance for adults is **no more than 14 units/week regularly**, spread over at least 3 days if drinking this amount. Discuss drink-free days where there is no dependence. Pregnancy advice is to avoid alcohol. Units do not reliably predict an individual's fitness to drive. [NHS alcohol units](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/alcohol-advice/calculating-alcohol-units/).

Use **AUDIT-C** for initial screening and a full AUDIT where indicated; a score of **≥5 on AUDIT-C** prompts fuller assessment. A full AUDIT score **≥20 suggests possible dependence**, requiring clinical assessment. CAGE can open a discussion but does not quantify current drinking or exclude harmful use.
## Alcohol dependence and withdrawal
Ask about morning drinking, drinking to relieve shaking, previous withdrawal fits/delirium, previous detoxifications, last drink and current tremor, sweating, nausea, agitation or hallucinations. Examine observations, hydration, nutrition and mental state; check for liver disease and other medical complications. Blood tests can assess complications but do not determine whether dependence is present.

**Do not advise abrupt unsupported cessation in a dependent drinker.** Arrange prompt alcohol-service assessment and a planned medically assisted withdrawal. Severe withdrawal, seizures, delirium or high risk of these requires hospital care; use a lower admission threshold with frailty, major comorbidity, poor support or young age. Pregnancy needs coordinated specialist care.

Specialist withdrawal treatment commonly uses a reducing benzodiazepine regimen with monitoring. Assess nutrition and provide **thiamine prophylaxis** according to risk/pathway. Suspected Wernicke's encephalopathy—confusion, ataxia or eye signs, often an incomplete presentation—needs emergency assessment and **parenteral thiamine**. [NICE CG100](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg100/chapter/Recommendations), [national alcohol treatment guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/clinical-guidelines-for-alcohol-treatment/10-pharmacological-interventions).

For harmful use without dependence, offer personalised feedback, a brief motivational intervention, an achievable reduction plan and follow-up. Explore triggers and alternatives. Where dependence is established, link withdrawal planning to psychological treatment and relapse prevention, including specialist consideration of acamprosate or naltrexone when appropriate. Treatment also needs practical support with housing, relationships and comorbid mental illness.

**Useful language:** “The shaking when you miss a drink suggests your body may have become dependent. Stopping suddenly could be dangerous. Let's arrange a supported plan so you can make this change safely.”
## Opioid use, dependence and withdrawal
Include heroin and prescribed codeine, tramadol, morphine or oxycodone. Establish route, source, usual dose, last dose, recent abstinence, concurrent sedatives and whether they are already receiving methadone/buprenorphine. Check the treatment service, dispensing pharmacy, supervision arrangements and missed doses rather than duplicating a prescription.

- **Overdose:** reduced consciousness and slow/absent breathing are an emergency. Call an ambulance, provide airway/breathing support and naloxone according to training/availability. Further observation is needed because respiratory depression can recur.
- **Withdrawal:** sweating, yawning, rhinorrhoea, cramps, diarrhoea, agitation and insomnia. Assess hydration, medical illness and mental health; arrange timely drug-service support. Pregnancy or significant complications needs urgent coordinated specialist advice.
- **Injecting risks:** ask about sharing equipment, wounds, fever and blood-borne viruses. Offer appropriate testing, hepatitis vaccination assessment and access to sterile equipment.

Explain that methadone or buprenorphine can support treatment, with choice, initiation and supervision determined by an experienced service. Do not independently restart the usual dose after missed treatment; loss of tolerance can make this unsafe.

**Harm reduction:** offer take-home naloxone and training through the local pathway; avoid using alone or mixing opioids with alcohol/benzodiazepines. Explain that tolerance falls after abstinence, detoxification or imprisonment, making a previous dose potentially fatal. Arrange practical and psychological support alongside medication. [NICE opioid detoxification guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg52/chapter/1-Guidance).
## Cannabis
Ask about frequency, potency, route, tobacco use, reason for use, dependence/withdrawal and effects on work, study, relationships, memory and mood. Explore anxiety, paranoia, hallucinations and suicidal thoughts; do not assume these are harmless intoxication symptoms.

Assess cough/breathlessness where relevant, driving and safeguarding. Offer a clear discussion of risks and a collaborative reduction/cessation plan, psychological support and drug-service referral when needed. Acute psychosis or immediate danger needs urgent mental health assessment. Persistent vomiting may require assessment for dehydration and other causes, including cannabis hyperemesis.
## Motivational consultation and close
Ask permission to discuss risks, reflect ambivalence and connect change to the patient's own goals. “What would be better in your life if this changed?” Agree one realistic next step and identify barriers. Avoid moralising or demanding a promise to stop.

Specify the referral, contact arrangements and review date. Give explicit return instructions for withdrawal, confusion, seizures, breathing problems or deteriorating mental health. Ensure the plan includes children/dependants and a safe way to contact the patient.
## Example consultation phrases
- **View of use:** “What are your thoughts about the amount of alcohol you drink?”
- **Function/trigger:** “What do you think is the reason for drinking?” / “What has made you seek help at this point?”
- **Withdrawal history:** “What happens when you don't drink for a couple of days?” Ask about actual experience; do not suggest an unsupported trial of stopping if dependence is possible.
- **Previous attempts:** “Have you tried to cut down before? What did you try, and how did it go?”
- **Other substances:** “People sometimes use alcohol alongside other drugs or painkillers. Could I ask whether you use anything else?”
- **CAGE — cut down:** “Have you ever felt you should cut down your drinking?”
- **CAGE — annoyed:** “Have people annoyed you by criticising your drinking?”
- **CAGE — guilty:** “Have you ever felt bad or guilty about your drinking?”
- **CAGE — eye-opener:** “Have you ever needed a drink first thing in the morning to steady yourself or get going?”
