## Mental-health consultation priorities
Establish symptoms, course, function, previous episodes/treatment and what the patient wants. Review physical causes, medicines, alcohol/drugs, trauma, relationships, finances, housing, work and dependants. Assess mental state throughout: engagement, speech, mood, thought, perception, cognition and insight, without turning it into an impersonal checklist.

## Immediate safety and self-harm
Ask directly and calmly about suicidal thoughts, intent, plans, access to means, recent acts, previous attempts, what has stopped them and available support. Assess self-neglect, psychosis/mania, intoxication, safeguarding and risk to others. Recent poisoning/injury may need urgent physical treatment even if the person feels well.

A person with immediate danger or unable to remain safe needs urgent emergency/crisis assessment and a safe handover; establish location, accompaniment and means reduction where appropriate. Do not leave the plan at “call someone if worse.” Collaboratively plan warning signs, coping/support contacts, access to urgent help and follow-up. Avoid risk-score labels or promises of safety as discharge criteria. [NICE NG225](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225/chapter/recommendations)

## Depression
Ask low mood and anhedonia, then sleep, appetite/weight, energy, concentration, guilt/hopelessness, psychomotor change and function. Check bipolar history (overactivity/disinhibition/reduced sleep), psychosis and physical contributors. PHQ9 supports assessment/monitoring; it does not replace a clinical diagnosis or risk assessment.

Less severe depression: shared options including guided self-help, behavioural activation/CBT and active monitoring. Do not routinely prescribe antidepressants first-line unless informed preference/context supports it. More severe illness may warrant medication, psychological therapy or combination, with urgent specialist input for psychosis or major safety concerns.

If prescribing an SSRI, discuss delay to benefit, early agitation/anxiety or suicidal thoughts, GI/sexual/sleep effects, interactions/bleeding and withdrawal. Choice depends on previous response, comorbidities, overdose risk, QT issues and preference; do not automatically choose the same SSRI for every patient. Review usually within two weeks, **one week for ages 18–25 or suicide-risk concern**. Treatment often continues at least six months after remission, with individual relapse prevention and gradual supported taper. [NICE NG222](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng222/chapter/Recommendations)

## Postnatal mood and psychosis
Differentiate transient baby blues, depression and postpartum psychosis. Ask sleep beyond infant demands, function, bonding, intrusive thoughts, intent, psychotic symptoms and safety of mother/baby. Explore partner support and previous severe mental illness. Suspected postpartum psychosis is an emergency requiring immediate specialist assessment, not routine postnatal-depression follow-up.

Pregnancy/breastfeeding treatment needs individual benefit–risk assessment; do not abruptly stop effective treatment or assume every medicine is forbidden. Involve perinatal services as indicated and plan practical support.

## Bipolar disorder and lithium
Mania/hypomania may involve reduced sleep, increased energy, pressured speech, disinhibition, spending/sexual risk and poor insight. Suspected mania or severe depression with risk needs urgent specialist assessment. Do not treat a possible bipolar depression as ordinary SSRI monotherapy without advice.

For established illness, review adherence, relapse plan, physical monitoring and family involvement with consent. Lithium requires a coordinated shared-care plan, appropriately timed levels (usually 12 hours post-dose), renal/thyroid/calcium monitoring and review of interactions. Vomiting/diarrhoea, dehydration, coarse tremor, ataxia or confusion can signal toxicity: withhold and seek urgent assessment/levels according to the toxicity pathway. NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs and diuretics can raise levels. Pregnancy plans need specialist advice; do not stop abruptly without a plan.

## Anxiety, panic and phobias
Clarify persistent worry versus discrete panic or trigger-specific fear, avoidance, function and physical symptoms. Exclude relevant medical/drug causes and acute cardiac/respiratory disease when appropriate; panic-like symptoms are not automatically panic disorder.

GAD: education, active monitoring/self-help and CBT/applied relaxation or suitable medication according to severity/preferences. Panic: CBT/self-help and appropriate SSRI options; avoid routine long-term benzodiazepines. Phobias: graded exposure/CBT. Explain therapy as learning skills to change patterns, not “it's all in your head.”

## OCD
Ask intrusive unwanted thoughts/images/urges, rituals/mental acts, time consumed, distress and impairment. Distinguish ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts from intent while assessing safety individually. Discuss CBT with exposure/response prevention and/or SSRI according to impairment and preference; specialist input for complexity, severe impairment or treatment failure. Avoid reinforcing rituals through unlimited reassurance.

## Psychosis
Explore voices/other perceptions, content and commands, beliefs, distress, insight, sleep, mood, substances, medicines and physical symptoms. Ask whether the person feels compelled to act. Do not confront delusions aggressively or endorse them; acknowledge distress and assess safety.

First-episode psychosis needs prompt specialist/early-intervention assessment. Immediate risk, severe self-neglect, mania, delirium or medical concern needs same-day emergency assessment. Consider infection, neurological illness, intoxication/withdrawal and steroids as well as psychiatric diagnoses. Explain a plan and safe transport; do not start a new antipsychotic casually without the appropriate specialist arrangement.

## PTSD
Ask about trauma sensitively without demanding a detailed retelling. Assess re-experiencing, avoidance, hyperarousal, mood/cognition, dissociation, substance use and function/safety. Offer trauma-focused CBT/appropriate EMDR pathway; treatment depends on time since event and individual needs. Do not use a rigid one-month rule to deny early support. Medication can be considered according to preference/context, not as a substitute for trauma care.

## Eating disorders
Ask restriction, bingeing, purging, exercise, body image, weight trajectory and physical symptoms. A normal BMI does not exclude serious disease. Assess pulse/BP including postural change, hydration, ECG/electrolytes where indicated, rapid loss, fainting, cardiac symptoms and suicide risk. Medical compromise requires urgent acute care; do not rely on BMI alone.

Refer promptly for specialist eating-disorder care with ongoing physical monitoring and nutrition/psychological support. Severe malnutrition creates refeeding risk. Avoid praising weight loss or framing recovery as simply “eat more.”

## Delirium and memory loss
**Acute/fluctuating change** in attention, awareness or cognition suggests delirium, including on top of dementia: urgent same-day medical assessment for infection, drugs, metabolic disturbance, hypoxia, pain/retention and other causes. Do not diagnose UTI from a positive dipstick alone in an older confused adult. Sedation/antipsychotics are not routine first-line management; Lewy-body/Parkinson disease adds risk.

**Gradual decline:** seek examples, timeline, collateral history with consent, ADL/medicine/financial/driving safety, sensory impairment, depression and reversible contributors. Appropriate examination, cognitive tool and bloods precede memory-service referral; a normal screening score does not exclude dementia. Discuss support, carer strain and advance planning without declaring incapacity from diagnosis alone.

## Insomnia
Clarify sleep onset, waking, early waking, non-restorative sleep and daytime impact. Ask routine/environment, shift work, substances, mood/mania, pain, nocturia, apnoea and restless legs. Sleepiness at the wheel requires explicit driving advice.

Agree a sleep diary, consistent routine and treatment of cause; persistent insomnia benefits from CBT-I rather than sleep-hygiene advice alone. Hypnotics, if justified, need short duration, lowest effective dose and dependency/falls/driving counselling. Do not issue repeated long-term prescriptions without review.
