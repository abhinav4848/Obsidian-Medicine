## Sexual history and discharge
Create privacy, check consent and use non-judgemental language. Explain why personal questions matter without apologising for every question. Ask symptoms (discharge, ulcers/rash, itch, lumps, dysuria, bleeding, pelvic/testicular pain), last contact, partner history, condom use, relevant exposure sites, pregnancy/contraception and previous tests/treatment. Sampling follows sexual practices and anatomy, not assumptions about identity.

Assess fever, pelvic pain/PID, acute scrotal pain/torsion, retention, pregnancy complications and safeguarding/coercion. Recent exposure may require time-sensitive HIV PEP or emergency contraception. Check how results/contact will be handled confidentially.

Discharge may be physiological, candidiasis, BV, STI, foreign body or cervical disease. Character alone cannot establish every diagnosis. Explain examination/swabs, including self-sampling options where suitable. Explore beliefs about infidelity, fertility and stigma; a positive test does not establish when or from whom infection was acquired.

## Management common to STIs
**Treat infection → partner notification → offer wider STI screening.** Explain transmission, adherence, sexual abstinence until the relevant treatment period is complete for patient/partners, symptoms have settled and infection-specific advice is met. Offer condoms and vaccination/prevention where indicated. Test-of-cure and retesting for reinfection are different tasks.

Partner notification through sexual health services can protect confidentiality and safety; explore domestic-abuse risk before asking someone to notify a partner. Provide a clear route for complications, persistent symptoms and follow-up results.

## Chlamydia
May be asymptomatic or cause discharge, dysuria, postcoital/intermenstrual bleeding, PID or epididymo-orchitis. Use NAAT at appropriate sites.

Uncomplicated infection: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for seven days is first-line unless contraindicated. Pregnancy needs an alternative regimen and appropriate follow-up. Advise no sex including oral sex until the course is completed and symptoms resolved, with partner treatment addressed; azithromycin has a different waiting interval. Routine test-of-cure is not required for every uncomplicated case; pregnancy, adherence, persistent symptoms or selected alternative regimens alter this. Offer reinfection retesting according to the 2026 pathway (2–6 months). [BASHH 2026](https://www.bashh.org/_userfiles/pages/files/national_guideline_for_the_management_of_infection_chlamydia_trachomatis_2026.pdf)

## Gonorrhoea
NAAT plus culture/susceptibility where appropriate, ideally before treatment. Sexual-health care is important for resistance, partner management and follow-up. Ceftriaxone 1 g IM single dose is usual first-line uncomplicated treatment; alternatives require current specialist guidance. Known ciprofloxacin susceptibility does not automatically make it the preferred first-line drug. Assess infection sites and complications; ocular disease needs urgent ophthalmology/sexual-health care. [BASHH 2025](https://www.bashh.org/_userfiles/pages/files/gc_guideline_2025_final.pdf)

## Syphilis
Primary disease may cause a painless indurated ulcer; secondary disease can cause systemic illness, nodes and a widespread rash including palms/soles. Latent, neurological and cardiovascular disease require different assessment. Arrange sexual-health evaluation and appropriate serology; pregnancy or neurological/ocular symptoms increases urgency. Penicillin treatment and follow-up depend on stage and site. Avoid casual alternative antibiotic regimens. Discuss partners and the possibility of a transient treatment reaction.

## Genital herpes
Painful grouped blisters/ulcers ± dysuria/systemic symptoms; swab appropriate lesions. Offer early oral antiviral for first episodes, analgesia, gentle care and topical anaesthetic if appropriate. Retention, severe illness, eye/neurological complications or pregnancy require urgent specialist advice.

Explain recurrence/latency and asymptomatic shedding without assigning blame. Avoid sex during lesions/prodrome; condoms reduce but do not eliminate transmission. Discuss suppressive treatment if recurrent disease is troublesome. New infection late in pregnancy needs urgent maternity/sexual-health input.

## Anogenital warts
Usually benign HPV lesions; inspect and reconsider atypical, pigmented, ulcerated or persistent lesions. Treatment depends on site, burden, pregnancy and preference: selected topical treatments or procedures through appropriate services. Explain recurrence and that visible wart removal does not instantly eliminate HPV. Do not equate genital warts with cancer; continue normal screening advice.

## Candidiasis
Typical itch/soreness and white discharge, but check alternatives, diabetes, antibiotics and pregnancy. Suitable topical azole or oral fluconazole treats uncomplicated non-pregnant disease; pregnancy generally needs local azole treatment rather than oral fluconazole. Avoid irritants/douching and explain that thrush is not usually an STI; partners do not routinely need treatment unless symptomatic.

Recurrent symptoms need confirmation/speciation and consideration of other vulval disease. Long-term suppression is a planned regimen after assessment, not repeated empirical single doses.

## Trichomoniasis and BV
**Trichomoniasis:** discharge may be yellow-green/frothy with itch/dysuria; confirm using appropriate testing and treat with an effective metronidazole regimen. Partner treatment and wider STI screen are required. Pregnancy and persistence affect management.

**BV:** altered flora, reduced lactobacilli with anaerobic overgrowth; often thin fishy-smelling discharge. Treat symptomatic disease with suitable metronidazole or local alternative, considering pregnancy and preference. It is not simply evidence of poor hygiene or infidelity. Recurrent disease and partner-management advice should follow current sexual-health guidance.
