## Rash and itch — consultation structure
Establish onset, progression, site/distribution, symmetry, itch/pain, blistering, discharge, bleeding and systemic symptoms. Ask about new medicines, contact products, occupation, travel, household contacts, atopy and autoimmune disease. Examine sufficiently to describe morphology rather than diagnosing from “a rash.” Check nails, scalp/mucosa and systemic signs when relevant.

Urgent concerns: sepsis, non-blanching illness, rapidly spreading painful skin change, widespread blistering/mucosal disease, erythroderma, eye involvement or suspected malignancy. Arrange in-person assessment when remote information is inadequate.

Explore sleep, confidence, mood, school/work, relationships, contagiousness fears and treatments actually used. For generalised itch without primary rash consider renal/hepatic, thyroid, haematological and medicine causes; order targeted tests rather than assuming psychological itch. Treat cause and symptoms: bland emollients, gentle products, nails/clothing/temperature advice. Antihistamines do not treat every cause of itch.
## Acne
Assess comedones/inflammatory lesions, nodules, scarring, distribution, previous courses and adherence. Explore PCOS features, medicines, cosmetics, pregnancy plans and psychological harm. Severe nodulocystic/scarring disease, diagnostic uncertainty or significant distress warrants dermatology consideration; acne fulminans/systemic illness needs urgent assessment.

Offer a suitable **12-week combination course**. Options depend on severity: topical adapalene/benzoyl peroxide, tretinoin/clindamycin or other guideline combinations; moderate/severe disease may require oral doxycycline or lymecycline with appropriate non-antibiotic topical treatment. Do not use oral/topical antibiotic monotherapy or combine topical and oral antibiotics. Review at 12 weeks; prolonged antibiotics beyond six months should be exceptional. Retinoids and tetracyclines are unsuitable in pregnancy/planning pregnancy.

Explain delayed benefit, irritation management, non-comedogenic products and avoiding picking. Discuss hormonal contraception if appropriate and desired. Isotretinoin is specialist prescribed with pregnancy prevention and mental/sexual-health assessment/monitoring; new significant symptoms need prompt contact with the treating team. [NICE NG198](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng198/chapter/Recommendations)
## Atopic eczema
Chronic relapsing inflammation; treatment controls it rather than guaranteeing a cure. Ask about triggers, sleep, infection, scratching, impact on family and **how much/where/how often** creams are applied. Explore steroid fear and practical barriers.

- Frequent leave-on emollients and soap substitution, including between flares. Warn about emollient residue on fabric increasing fire risk.
- Topical steroid potency selected for age, site and severity; explain duration, application and a flare plan. Face/folds require particular caution. Demonstrate a fingertip unit rather than prescribing “a little.” One adult FTU covers roughly two adult handprints; body/age charts guide quantities.
- Do not recommend exclusion diets routinely; investigate suspected food allergy appropriately. Specialist options include calcineurin inhibitors, wet wraps and systemic therapies.
- Weeping/crusting alone does not prove bacterial infection. If not systemically unwell, antibiotics are not routinely needed; continue eczema treatment and assess extent/severity. [NICE NG190](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng190/chapter/Recommendations)
- **Eczema herpeticum:** rapidly painful worsening eczema with clustered vesicles/punched-out lesions, fever or malaise → immediate same-day specialist assessment; eye involvement needs urgent ophthalmology. Do not wait for a swab before escalation.
## Psoriasis
Assess extent, site, symptoms, nails, joint pain/swelling and function, not simply plaque size. Ask about recent streptococcal illness, trauma, stress and medicines such as lithium/beta-blockers. Explore mood, stigma and alcohol/smoking. Generalised pustular or erythrodermic psoriasis needs same-day specialist help.

Explain accelerated skin turnover/inflammation and relapsing course. Use emollients and site-appropriate topical corticosteroid/vitamin-D regimens with defined review and safe treatment breaks. Face/flexures differ from trunk/limbs. Difficult/high-impact disease may need phototherapy, systemic medicines or biologics. Suspected psoriatic arthritis needs rheumatology referral; do not wait for severe skin disease.
## Pigment conditions
- **Vitiligo:** sharply defined depigmented, non-scaly patches; assess distribution, autoimmune context and emotional impact. Offer sun protection/camouflage and discuss dermatology treatment options; avoid promises about progression or cure.
- **Pityriasis versicolor:** fine-scaled pigment change, often trunk and worse in warm weather; usually not contagious. Topical antifungal treatment is usual; pigment recovery can lag behind clearance.
- **Pityriasis rosea:** herald patch followed by oval trunk lesions; usually self-limiting over weeks. Treat itch if needed and reconsider atypical disease. Pregnancy merits specialist advice, especially early gestation.
## Ulcers

| Pattern | Assessment and action |
|---|---|
| Venous: gaiter region, oedema/skin staining | Wound care and compression after adequate arterial assessment; manage venous disease |
| Arterial: painful distal/punched-out lesion, cold foot/poor pulses | Urgent vascular assessment if limb-threatening; do not apply routine high compression |
| Neuropathic: pressure point, reduced sensation, sometimes little pain | Offloading, foot protection and urgent diabetic-foot pathway when applicable |
Check pain, infection, vascular supply, neuropathy and possible malignancy in an atypical/non-healing ulcer. Antibiotics are for clinical infection, not colonisation. ABPI can be misleading in diabetes/calcified vessels; use skilled vascular assessment. See [[CVS#PAD and limb ischaemia]].
## Cellulitis and impetigo
**Cellulitis:** spreading erythema, warmth, pain and swelling; identify entry site and differentiate DVT, venous eczema and septic joint. Assess systemic illness, rate of spread, disproportionate pain and immunosuppression. Oral flucloxacillin is usual first-line uncomplicated treatment; choose dose/course from current local guidance and review response. Eye/nose involvement, severe illness or necrotising features needs urgent specialist assessment. Mark the edge, elevate, give analgesia and explain that worsening/spread or systemic illness warrants reassessment.

**Impetigo:** honey-coloured crusting or bullous lesions. Localised non-bullous disease may use hydrogen peroxide 1% cream; suitable topical antibiotic if inappropriate/ineffective. Widespread/bullous disease or systemic risk may require oral treatment. Avoid combining oral and topical antibiotics routinely. Discuss hand hygiene, separate towels and school exclusion until lesions crust/heal or 48 hours after antibiotics begin. Recurrent/persistent disease may need swabs and reassessment.
## Fungal skin and nail infection
Site names: capitis=scalp, barbae=beard, faciei=face, manuum=hand, pedis=foot, cruris=groin, corporis=body, unguium=nail. Look for annular scale, interdigital fissuring and relevant moisture/contact history. Consider psoriasis/eczema and steroid-modified tinea incognito.

Use topical antifungal for suitable limited skin disease; scalp infection needs systemic treatment and appropriate specialist/paediatric advice. Confirm suspected fungal nails with mycology before oral treatment. Discuss lengthy courses, limited cosmetic response, footwear/hygiene and oral-drug interactions/liver monitoring. Do not equate every thickened nail with fungus.
## Blisters, nodules and lumps
- **Pemphigus/pemphigoid:** assess painful versus itchy, flaccid versus tense blisters, mucosa, extent and systemic compromise. New widespread blistering needs urgent dermatology; biopsy/immunofluorescence guides specialist treatment.
- **Erythema nodosum:** tender shin nodules; consider infection, sarcoidosis, IBD, pregnancy and medicines. Investigate the context rather than labelling a skin-only disorder.
- **Ganglion:** assess location, pain, function and diagnostic confidence. Explain a benign fluid-filled lump; observation is reasonable if uncomplicated. Persistent troublesome or uncertain lesions need review; do not advise striking it.
## Skin cancer and changing lesions
Ask about evolution, bleeding, ulceration, itch/sensation, previous cancer, immunosuppression, UV/sunbed exposure and family history. Examine the whole lesion with suitable lighting/dermoscopy expertise; assess nodes when indicated. Explain the reason for referral without declaring unconfirmed cancer.

| Lesion | Core action |
|---|---|
| Actinic keratosis | Sun-damaged rough/scaly lesion; assess for SCC features, choose lesion/field treatment appropriately |
| BCC | Pearly/rolled-edge or non-healing lesion; usually routine referral, but site/size/risk may justify urgent care |
| Suspected SCC | Urgent suspected-cancer pathway |
| Bowen disease | Intraepidermal SCC; dermatology/local pathway for treatment and exclusion of invasion |
| Suspicious melanoma | Urgent suspected-cancer referral; do not delay to photograph/watch a concerning lesion |
Weighted seven-point melanoma checklist: major features (size change, irregular shape, irregular colour) score 2 each; minor (diameter ≥7 mm, inflammation, oozing, sensation change) score 1. Score ≥3 supports urgent referral, but a lower score does not overrule clinical concern. Nodular melanoma may not follow classic ABCDE. Discuss clinic assessment, possible excision/biopsy and support, avoiding promises about stage.
## Hair loss and infestations
- **Alopecia areata:** smooth patches, possible exclamation-mark hairs/nail pitting; distinguish scarring, tinea and diffuse shedding. Explore autoimmune context and distress; treatment/referral depends on extent and impact. Exclamation-mark hairs indicate activity, not proof of recovery.
- **Head lice:** confirm live lice; offer wet combing or suitable treatment with correct repeat schedule. Check close contacts and treat affected people. It is not evidence of poor hygiene.
- **Scabies:** nocturnal itch, contacts and typical burrows/distribution. Treat close/household/sexual contacts simultaneously and apply permethrin 5% correctly with repeat after one week, adapting application for age. Address clothes/bedding and explain itch may persist for weeks. Crusted scabies needs specialist/infection-control management.
## Lyme disease
Ask about outdoor/tick exposure and neurological, cardiac or joint symptoms. Typical erythema migrans is a clinical diagnosis: treat without waiting for serology. Antibiotic choice/duration depends on age, pregnancy and manifestations; uncomplicated adult disease commonly uses doxycycline for 21 days. Seek specialist advice for complications, not automatically for every uncomplicated case. [NICE NG95](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng95/chapter/Recommendations)
## Example consultation phrases
- **Describe the rash:** “Let me ask a few questions about the rash so we can work out what's going on. Where exactly is it?” (V16, 01:02)
- **Distribution without jargon:** “Is it on the inside of the elbow, or mainly on the outside?” (V16, 01:43)
- **Relevant context:** “Skin problems can sometimes be linked to things in our lives. Can I ask a few questions about that?” (V16, 05:13)
- **Acne and periods:** “Acne can be linked to the menstrual cycle. Do you mind if I ask a few questions about your periods?” (V16, 23:48)
- **Eczema and cure:** “Eczema is a long-term condition that makes the skin dry, cracked, red and sore. We cannot promise a cure, but there are effective ways to control it.” (V17, 03:27)
- **Emollients:** “It's important to use plenty of moisturiser regularly, including when the skin is doing better.” (V17, 05:06)
- **Steroid amounts:** “Let's go through how much steroid cream to use so your child gets the right amount.” Use the age/body-site fingertip-unit advice in the clinical section. (V17, 06:59)
- **Psoriasis:** “It's a long-term condition that can come and go in flare-ups. The immune system causes inflammation in the skin, which leads to the soreness, itching and scaling.” (V17, 11:40)
- **Associated symptoms:** “Psoriasis can sometimes affect other parts of the body. Could I ask about your nails and joints?” (V17, 13:14)
- **Vitiligo testing:** “We usually diagnose this by examining the skin. Sometimes we investigate other conditions that can be associated with it.” Choose tests from the history, rather than promising every autoimmune test. (V17, 16:52)
- **Prognosis when uncertain:** “Everyone is different, and it isn't possible to predict exactly how it will change for you.” (V17, 17:57)
- **Ulcer:** “It's a break in the skin that is taking a long time to heal.” (V17, 18:32)
- **Venous ulcer:** “The veins aren't returning blood up the leg as well as they should. That raises pressure in the lower leg and makes healing more difficult.” (V17, 19:05)
- **Cellulitis:** “It's an infection in the skin, usually after bacteria enter through a break in it.” (V17, 24:48)
- **Fungal terminology:** “Tinea means a fungal skin infection. The second word tells us where it is—for example, pedis means the feet and capitis the scalp.” (V18, 00:39)
- **Ganglion:** “It's a fluid-filled swelling arising near a joint or tendon sheath, commonly at the wrist.” (V18, 10:06)
- **Specialist assessment:** “They may examine it with a dermatoscope, which lets them look at the skin in more detail. A tissue sample may be needed to establish what it is.” (V18, 21:30)
