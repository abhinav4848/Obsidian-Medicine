## Cough and breathlessness — establish severity
Ask onset/progression, cough/sputum/haemoptysis, dyspnoea, chest pain, fever, weight/appetite, hoarseness and swallowing. Consider cardiac causes as well as respiratory: orthopnoea, PND and oedema matter. Review smoking/vaping, medicines (including ACE inhibitor), atopy, TB/travel/VTE risks, occupational dust/chemicals, mould/birds and changes away from work.

Assess speech, effort, observations, oxygenation and relevant examination. Severe breathlessness, hypoxia, collapse/confusion, significant bleeding or suspected PE/pneumothorax/ACS/sepsis needs urgent action. Explore activity, work, sleep, support and the patient's actual worry/request.

## Acute cough, URTI and pneumonia
Explain that uncomplicated viral cough can take several weeks; coloured sputum alone does not prove bacterial infection. Offer symptom care and specific deterioration advice; avoid routine antibiotics or scald-risk steam inhalation.

For suspected pneumonia, combine clinical judgement with **CRB65**: confusion, RR ≥30, systolic BP <90 or diastolic ≤60, age ≥65 (one each). Score 0 often suits primary care; 1 needs individual choice among supported community/same-day/hospital care; ≥2 consider hospital. Sepsis/cardiorespiratory failure or inability to take treatment overrides the score. CURB65 adds urea >7 for hospital assessment.

Antibiotics depend on severity, allergy and local guidance; uncomplicated adult low-severity CAP commonly uses amoxicillin for five days. Explain expected improvement, reassessment if not starting to improve in about three days, and follow-up imaging where indicated by symptoms/risk. [NICE NG250](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng250/chapter/recommendations)

## Suspected asthma
Look for variable wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness/cough, night/exercise symptoms, triggers and atopy; consider alternatives and occupational pattern. Confirm objectively where possible and document “suspected” until supported. Treat acute illness first rather than delaying urgent care for testing.

Age-specific diagnostic sequence differs: adults ≥17 start with eosinophils/FeNO (FeNO ≥50 ppb), then bronchodilator reversibility, PEF variability and specialist challenge if needed; children 5–16 start FeNO (≥35), then other objective testing per pathway. Under-fives usually need a monitored treatment trial and reassessment. Steroid treatment can normalise tests, so interpret negative results in context.

## Asthma maintenance and review
Every review: symptoms/night waking, activity/school/work, reliever use/prescription history, exacerbations/steroid courses, adherence, technique/device and triggers. Provide a written personalised action plan and demonstration/teach-back.

- **Age ≥12:** as-needed low-dose ICS/formoterol (AIR) is an initial option; uncontrolled disease → low-dose then moderate-dose MART. Markedly symptomatic initial presentation may require starting MART. SABA alone is not appropriate maintenance care.
- Uncontrolled on moderate MART: check FeNO/eosinophils; raised markers favour specialist referral, otherwise consider suitable LTRA/LAMA trial with timed review. Montelukast needs neuropsychiatric adverse-effect counselling.
- **Age 5–11:** paediatric low-dose ICS with SABA reliever initially; later options include paediatric MART when suitable or an alternative step-up pathway. Refer if uncontrolled despite appropriate treatment. See [[asthma- management age 5-11]].
- **Under 5:** time-limited ICS trial plus reliever and review of response, technique/triggers and alternative diagnosis; persistent/severe/recurrent disease needs specialist input.

Use correct product/device licences and age doses; not every ICS/LABA is an AIR/MART inhaler. Review treatment changes at 8–12 weeks or sooner as clinically needed; step down cautiously after sustained control. Pregnancy requires good control and appropriate treatment, not stopping inhalers. [BTS/NICE/SIGN NG245](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng245/chapter/recommendations)

## Acute asthma
Assess by the **worst feature**, not PEF alone. Adult acute severe features include PEF 33–50% best/predicted, RR ≥25, pulse ≥110 or inability to complete sentences. Life-threatening features include PEF <33%, SpO2 <92%, silent chest, poor effort, exhaustion, altered consciousness, hypotension/arrhythmia or cyanosis.

Urgent emergency transfer for life-threatening features or inadequate response/severe concern. Give appropriate inhaled bronchodilator via spacer/nebuliser, controlled oxygen where indicated and systemic steroid without delaying transfer. Adult prednisolone is commonly 40–50 mg daily for at least five days; child doses differ. Severe attacks may need ipratropium and hospital escalation. Arrange early post-attack review, technique/action-plan check and prevention review. [BTS/SIGN acute pathway](https://www.rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/asthma-pathway-bts-nice-sign-sign-244/managing-acute-asthma/management-of-acute-asthma-in-adults/asthma-management-algorithms-for-adults/management-of-acute-asthma-in-adults-in-general-practice/management-of-acute-asthma-in-adults-in-general-practice/)

## COPD diagnosis and stable review
Assess smoking/exposures, chronic dyspnoea, cough/sputum, exacerbations and functional limitation. Confirm with appropriate post-bronchodilator spirometry; ratio FEV1/FVC <0.7 supports obstruction, interpreted with age/context. Consider asthma, HF, cancer, bronchiectasis and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency where indicated. Clubbing is not a routine COPD finding.

Management: smoking cessation, vaccination, exercise/pulmonary rehabilitation, nutrition, inhaler technique and comorbidity care. Short-acting reliever then long-acting treatment according to symptoms/exacerbations and asthmatic/steroid-responsive features. LABA/LAMA suits many; ICS-containing treatment is selected, not automatic. Assess benefit/risks before triple therapy. Agree action plan and review rather than adding inhalers without checking use.

## COPD exacerbation
Compare with baseline and consider pneumonia, PE, pneumothorax or HF. Assess RR/SpO2, work of breathing, confusion, cyanosis, cardiac symptoms, prior admissions and home support. Severe deterioration or unsafe home care warrants hospital.

Optimise short-acting bronchodilator; suitable adult steroid course is prednisolone 30 mg/day for five days. Antibiotics are selective, considering sputum change, severity, history and resistance; not every exacerbation is bacterial. Controlled oxygen often targets 88–92% in those at risk of hypercapnic failure, with assessment/blood gases. NIV is a hospital decision. Rescue packs require education and a review plan. [NICE NG115](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng115/chapter/Recommendations)

## PE and anticoagulation
Ask dyspnoea, pleuritic pain, haemoptysis, syncope, leg symptoms and VTE risks. Unstable or high clinical concern → emergency pathway. In stable non-pregnant adults use appropriate probability assessment: PE Wells >4 leads to prompt CTPA; ≤4 to D-dimer, with interim anticoagulation when investigation is delayed according to pathway. Pregnancy follows its own urgent pathway.

Explain clot, investigations and treatment; anticoagulation usually lasts at least three months, then review cause/recurrence/bleeding risk. Do not automatically order extensive occult-cancer tests for every unprovoked PE. Discuss bleeding, interactions, adherence, travel and follow-up. See [[CVS#DVT and swollen legs]].

## Pneumothorax and pleural effusion
Sudden pleuritic pain/dyspnoea with unilateral reduced sounds can indicate pneumothorax. Tension physiology/shock is an emergency requiring immediate trained emergency treatment, not waiting for X-ray. Stable treatment depends on symptoms, underlying disease and imaging; observation, ambulatory care, aspiration or drain are specialist decisions.

Pleural effusion can be cardiac, malignant, infective or other disease. New/unexplained effusion needs investigation; severe breathlessness/sepsis increases urgency. Explain imaging, ultrasound-guided sampling/drainage and cause-directed treatment, avoiding the assumption that fluid always means cancer. Flying/diving advice after pneumothorax needs the current specialist guidance.

## Lung cancer, clubbing and ILD
Persistent/new respiratory symptoms, haemoptysis, weight loss, hoarseness, nodes, clubbing or concerning examination needs cancer consideration. Age ≥40 unexplained haemoptysis or suspicious imaging supports urgent suspected-cancer referral; urgent CXR criteria depend on age, symptoms and smoking history. A normal CXR does not override strong ongoing suspicion.

Clubbing can reflect pulmonary, GI, cardiac or other disease; identify cause rather than diagnosing cancer from the sign alone. ILD may cause progressive exertional dyspnoea, dry cough and fine inspiratory crackles; assess drugs, exposures and connective-tissue disease. Spirometry alone cannot confirm restriction—lung volumes/HRCT and specialist work-up may be needed.

## Bronchiectasis
Think recurrent infections, chronic productive cough, haemoptysis and crackles; HRCT establishes diagnosis. Specialist plan includes airway-clearance physiotherapy, sputum-guided treatment, vaccinations and cause assessment. Exacerbations often need longer antibiotic courses than simple LRTI. Significant haemoptysis or systemic deterioration requires urgent care.

## OSA and long-term oxygen
OSA: snoring, witnessed apnoea, unrefreshing sleep, daytime sleepiness and work/driving impact. Use symptoms plus appropriate tools to support sleep-service referral; CPAP, weight measures or mandibular devices depend on assessment. **Unsafe sleepiness means stop driving pending control/advice**; DVLA rules depend on sleepiness, diagnosis and licence group.

LTOT is for selected documented chronic hypoxaemia after optimisation, not breathlessness alone. COPD assessment uses stable blood gases; smoking/fire risk is central. NICE advises not offering LTOT to people who continue smoking despite cessation support. Do not recommend routine home oxygen for non-hypoxaemic HF or palliative breathlessness. [NICE NG115](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng115/chapter/Recommendations)

## Smoking cessation
Ask what/how much, dependence, triggers, previous attempts and household support. Agree a quit plan and behavioural support plus suitable medicine: combination NRT, varenicline, cytisinicline or bupropion depending on eligibility/preferences/current local access. Varenicline is available in the UK; check local access and individual suitability. Explain withdrawal, relapse as a chance to adjust support, and benefits at any age. Vaping is less harmful than smoking but not risk-free; discuss complete switching and avoiding dual use in the relevant cessation plan. [NHS medication advice](https://www.nhsinform.scot/healthy-living/stopping-smoking/how-to-stop/stop-smoking-medication/)
