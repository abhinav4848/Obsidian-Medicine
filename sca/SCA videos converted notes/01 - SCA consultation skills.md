## Exam and preparation
Twelve simulated remote consultations, each 12 minutes with three minutes' reading time. Cases involve patients, carers or professionals by video or audio. See [[SCA mock exam framework]] for the domains: data gathering/diagnosis, clinical management/medical complexity, relating to others. Examination performance is assessed elsewhere, but interpreting supplied findings and arranging examination remain important.

Read for identity, setting, task, prior events, medicines, results and gaps. Decide where you join the patient's journey. Prepare urgent decisions without locking into a diagnosis; reset after each case.

## Open and focus
Let the opening statement finish; pause for extra information. Use a focused open invitation, then targeted questions. New unexplained symptoms need history from onset; reviews need current change, control and patient agenda rather than a complete retelling.

Use relevant SOCRATES elements for pain: site, onset, character, radiation, associations, timing, aggravating/relieving factors, severity/function. For other symptoms establish onset, duration, progression, triggers, relief and associations. Use records actively: “I see you started this last week; how have you been getting on?”

## Three areas to explore
### Safety
Identify action needed now, today or urgently. Signpost specific warning questions, avoid leading negatives and follow positive findings promptly. Include safeguarding, driving, machinery, medicine risk and dependants where relevant. Mnemonics do not replace judgement.

### Life and practical context
Explore how life affects illness **and** illness affects life. Work, alcohol, smoking and home are prompts adapted to relevance; add sleep, stress, diet, activity, travel, recreational drugs and sexual context as indicated.
- Home: actual support, ADLs and caring duties, not just who lives there.
- Work: duties, hazards, impact, employment status and affordability of time off.
- Habits: amount, reasons, previous attempts and readiness for support.
Follow through answers: “How has that affected work?”

### Understanding, concerns and goals
Ask “What have you been thinking?” “What worries you most?” “What were you hoping we could achieve?” Allow concerns about work or family as well as diagnosis. Reflect important answers. If a concern is already stated, acknowledge it and ask whether anything else matters. Do not repeat questions as if you have not listened.

## Relevant background
Use PMH, drugs, allergies, family history and systems review selectively. When important, ask properly: actual drug use, OTC/herbal/injected treatments, contraception, adverse effects and what an alleged allergy involved. Explore disease-specific family history; avoid a routine tour of every organ system.

## Diagnosis and explanation
State the working conclusion and reasoning. If uncertain, explain the leading possibilities and how you will clarify them. Ask what the patient knows and whether an explanation would help. Explain in two or three plain sentences, then check understanding. Give results promptly enough to meet the patient's needs; do not delay by a fixed formula.

## Management
1. Give the headline: diagnosis or working differential.
2. Tailor explanation to existing understanding.
3. Bring together clinical needs, worries, function and practical barriers.
4. Lead when safety requires it; otherwise agree the priority.
5. Discuss realistic choices, benefits/harms and preferences; agree who does what and when.

For example: “We need to treat the attacks, look at the work trigger and discuss your scan concern. First I need to check you today; then we can work through longer-term options.”

Destinations: emergency transfer, same-day assessment/admission, urgent referral, routine referral, planned examination/review or remote care. Listing issues does not substitute for addressing them.

## Relating to others
**Reasoning:** explain unexpected questions. **Relevance:** choose questions for this person. **Reflection:** respond to answers and emotion rather than saying “okay” and moving on.

Acknowledge cues now and return appropriately: “Work sounds important; I'll come back after checking these symptoms.” Actually return. Use silence thoughtfully; describe emotion tentatively without claiming to know exactly how someone feels. Chunk information and invite questions or teach-back.

## Closing and recovery when stuck
Agree actions, timing, responsibility and what to do if the plan fails. Where deterioration is possible, explain specific warning symptoms, urgency and contact route. A planned safety-net must actually be delivered. Arrange follow-up and check understanding.

If blank: pause, check safety, life context and the main concern; identify the next action. A short summary can restore direction. Arrange concrete follow-up for unfinished issues after checking they can safely wait.
