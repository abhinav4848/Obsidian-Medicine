## Neurological assessment
Establish precise onset, progression, distribution, weakness/sensation, vision, speech, gait, headache and sphincter symptoms. Acute focal deficit, severe sudden headache, altered consciousness, meningism, progressive respiratory weakness or spinal-emergency features needs urgent action.

Use supplied examination findings meaningfully. UMN patterns often include increased tone/brisk reflexes/upgoing plantar; LMN patterns reduced tone/reflexes with wasting/fasciculation, interpreted in context. MRC power: 0 none; 1 contraction; 2 movement with gravity eliminated; 3 against gravity; 4 against resistance but weak; 5 normal.

Ask about medicines, toxins/alcohol, diabetes, malignancy, infection, family history and function. Driving, heights, water, machinery and vulnerable dependants can be central to the plan. Describe necessary in-person assessment rather than pretending a full remote neurological examination excludes disease.
## Headache
Characterise onset/time to peak, duration, frequency, site, severity, triggers, aura, autonomic features and analgesic days. Ask about pregnancy/postpartum state, trauma, fever, neurological/visual symptoms, cancer/immunosuppression, postural/exertional triggers and similar illness in the household (CO exposure).

**Urgent:** thunderclap onset, meningism/systemic illness, focal deficit, altered consciousness, papilloedema/raised-ICP concern, acute painful visual loss or suspected GCA. Arrange the relevant emergency/same-day assessment. Do not reassure from the label “migraine” if the pattern has changed significantly.

| Pattern | Treatment discussion |
|---|---|
| Migraine: disabling episodic pain ± nausea, photo/phonophobia, aura | Suitable triptan plus NSAID/paracetamol, antiemetic when appropriate; consider preventive treatment by impact/preferences |
| Tension type: often bilateral pressure, less disabling | Suitable simple analgesia, trigger/function discussion; avoid opioids |
| Cluster: severe unilateral orbital pain with autonomic signs/restlessness | Rapid acute treatment with high-flow oxygen and/or nasal/subcutaneous triptan; specialist prevention plan |
| Medication-overuse | Explain the cycle and supported withdrawal/review; strategy differs with opioids/dependence |
Use a diary and avoid excessive acute-treatment days. Preventive migraine options include propranolol, amitriptyline or topiramate, chosen for comorbidity/safety; topiramate needs current Pregnancy Prevention Programme restrictions. Aura affects combined hormonal contraceptive suitability. Chronic migraine is ≥15 headache days/month for >3 months with migraine features on ≥8 days, not simply “more than 15 migraines.” Specialist options exist after appropriate treatment failure. [NICE CG150](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg150/chapter/recommendations)
## GCA and intracranial bleeding
GCA: new headache in an older adult, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, constitutional symptoms or PMR. Check transient/permanent visual symptoms. Start appropriate high-dose steroid treatment promptly and arrange same-day specialist pathway; visual symptoms require emergency ophthalmic assessment. Do not await inflammatory tests or biopsy before treating a convincing case.

Thunderclap headache suggests SAH and requires emergency assessment even if pain improves. Trauma plus progressive headache/confusion, especially on anticoagulants or in older adults, raises subdural bleeding. Hospital CT/LP decisions depend on timing and findings; do not promise every negative CT needs LP.
## Head injury
Ask mechanism/time, loss of consciousness/amnesia, vomiting, seizure, neurological symptoms, anticoagulants/antiplatelets, intoxication and neck injury. Reduced consciousness, seizure, focal deficit, suspected skull fracture or high-risk trauma needs emergency transfer. Other risk factors warrant ED assessment/imaging under the current algorithm.

Anticoagulants or antiplatelets other than aspirin alone can justify CT even without other indications. Do not dismiss an apparently minor injury in these patients. For safe discharge, provide written/verbal deterioration advice, responsible adult supervision and activity/driving advice. [NICE NG232](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng232/chapter/recommendations)
## Stroke and TIA
**Current focal symptoms:** record last known well, FAST and other symptoms, glucose if available without delaying help; emergency stroke transfer. Do not give routine aspirin for undifferentiated ongoing stroke before haemorrhage is excluded. Explain urgent imaging and possible reperfusion assessment without promising eligibility.

**Fully resolved focal episode:** suspected TIA needs immediate appropriate referral and aspirin 300 mg unless contraindicated. Do not use ABCD2 to delay assessment; urgent specialist assessment is usually within 24 hours. Recurrent/crescendo episodes, ongoing deficits or particular bleeding/anticoagulant issues require immediate stroke-team advice. Old presentations still need prompt assessment, not routine reassurance. [National stroke guideline](https://www.strokeguideline.org/chapter/acute-care/)

Post-stroke/TIA review: antiplatelet versus anticoagulant depends on mechanism; statin, BP and other risk treatment, rehabilitation, swallowing/nutrition, communication, continence, fatigue, cognition/mood and carer support. Discuss current DVLA group-specific rules and return to work. Carotid imaging/intervention depends on specialist findings.
## First seizure and epilepsy
Obtain **before–during–after** history, witness/video if available, duration, recovery, tongue injury, triggers and previous subtle episodes. Consider syncope/arrhythmia, metabolic disturbance, intoxication/withdrawal, infection and structural disease. Check glucose, ECG and appropriate clinical assessment.

Ongoing seizure ≥5 minutes, recurrent seizures without recovery, persistent altered state, injury, fever/meningism, focal deficit or other acute concern → emergency care. Protect from injury, time the event, no restraint/nothing in the mouth; recovery position once appropriate and follow a prescribed rescue plan if trained. A recovered stable person after a first suspected seizure needs urgent specialist assessment, generally within two weeks; not every first seizure automatically requires admission.

A first seizure does not automatically establish epilepsy. Explain EEG/imaging and that a normal EEG does not exclude it. Until assessed, stop driving and follow notification advice; discuss bathing/swimming, heights, machinery and support. For established epilepsy review adherence, triggers, rescue plan, pregnancy/contraception and drug interactions. Valproate restrictions apply beyond a simple “women only” rule; use current specialist/MHRA requirements. Do not stop antiseizure medicine abruptly. [NICE NG217](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng217/chapter/Recommendations)
## Tremor and Parkinsonism
Characterise rest, postural or action/intention tremor; assess bradykinesia, rigidity, gait, medicines, thyroid symptoms and alcohol. A tremor alone does not diagnose Parkinson disease. Suspected Parkinson disease should be referred promptly **untreated** for specialist assessment.

For known disease address medication timing, wearing-off/dyskinesia, falls, swallowing, constipation, sleep, cognition, hallucinations, mood, postural symptoms and carer burden. Dopamine agonists can affect impulse control and alertness. Avoid abrupt interruption of dopaminergic treatment; seek specialist advice for changes.
## Falls, blackouts and postural symptoms
Reconstruct before/during/after, witness account, injury and recovery. Look for arrhythmia, seizure, stroke, postural hypotension, medicines, infection, vision and environmental factors. Exertional syncope, cardiac symptoms/abnormal ECG, family sudden death or poor recovery requires urgent assessment.

Recurrent falls need multifactorial assessment: lying/standing BP, medication review, gait/balance, vision, cognition, feet/footwear, bone health, home hazards and fear of falling. Physio, OT, home adaptations and alarm/support plans may help. Explain actions, not just a generic falls-clinic referral.
## Peripheral and autonomic neuropathy
Map distribution and progression; distinguish nerve entrapment, length-dependent neuropathy and spinal disease. Assess diabetes, B12, alcohol, renal/thyroid disease, medicines and systemic causes. Rapid progression, motor/bulbar or autonomic instability needs urgent review. Target tests and nerve studies as indicated; do not stop an essential drug such as phenytoin casually.

Treat cause, protect insensate feet and address function. Neuropathic-pain options (excluding trigeminal neuralgia and separate sciatica guidance) include amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin, selected/titrated and reviewed for benefit/harms. Avoid automatic combination/escalation without reassessment; dependence/withdrawal and renal dosing matter.
## MS, MND, myasthenia and GBS

| Presentation | SCA priorities |
|---|---|
| MS: episodes of focal neurological symptoms, optic neuritis or sensory/motor/gait change | Specialist diagnosis; urgent review for significant new deficits. Distinguish relapse from infection and do not start steroids indiscriminately |
| MND: progressive weakness, wasting/fasciculation ± UMN/bulbar signs | Prompt specialist referral; discuss communication, nutrition, respiratory support, function and goals sensitively |
| Myasthenia: fatigable ocular/bulbar/limb weakness | Specialist antibodies/neurophysiology; breathing or swallowing deterioration is an emergency |
| GBS: progressive weakness/areflexia, often after infection | Urgent hospital assessment for respiratory/autonomic monitoring; do not wait for outpatient tests |
Cerebellar features include ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus and intention tremor; acute onset needs vascular consideration. Bulbar symptoms create swallowing/aspiration risk regardless of cause.
## Facial palsy and neuralgia
New facial weakness requires assessment for stroke and other causes. Bell palsy affects the whole side including eye closure, but forehead involvement alone is not a perfect exclusion test for central disease. Typical early Bell palsy may benefit from oral steroids within 72 hours, with eye protection/lubrication and follow-up. Atypical features, ear vesicles, recurrence or poor recovery need specialist assessment.

Trigeminal neuralgia: brief severe electric-shock facial pain triggered by touch/chewing; consider dental and secondary causes. Carbamazepine is usual first-line with interaction/safety review; atypical neurological features or inadequate control need specialist assessment.
## Brain tumour concern
Progressive subacute loss of central neurological function needs urgent investigation/referral; adult direct-access MRI (CT if unsuitable) and paediatric pathways differ. Seizures, raised ICP or acute deterioration may require emergency care. Explore fear and practical support while explaining what testing can establish.
## Example consultation phrases
- **Headache examination:** “An examination can tell us a lot about a headache. I'd like to check your blood pressure, look at the back of your eyes and examine your nervous system.” (V40, 16:12)
- **Medication-overuse headache:** “When painkillers are used very frequently, they can end up keeping headaches going. Headaches may temporarily worsen when we stop the overused medicine, so let's agree a supported plan.” (V40, 23:25–24:55)
- **Stroke mechanisms:** “A stroke can happen when a blood vessel supplying the brain is blocked. Some strokes are caused by bleeding in the brain instead.” (V41, 14:39)
- **Secondary prevention:** “These medicines help reduce the chance of another stroke. The choice depends on what caused your stroke and your other health risks.” (V41, 19:24)
- **After stroke:** “How is your mood these days?” / “Now that you've had a stroke, we need to talk about driving. Has anyone discussed that with you?” (V41, 20:45–21:30)
- **Recurrent TIA symptoms:** “You mentioned losing power in your leg yesterday. Has that, or any of the other symptoms we've discussed, happened on other occasions recently?” (V41, 25:02)
- **Carotid operation:** “In some people, an operation to remove narrowing in the neck artery can reduce future stroke risk. We need imaging and a specialist assessment to see whether it would help you.” (V41, 26:08)
- **Seizure:** “A seizure is a sudden burst of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. That seems to fit the symptoms your wife described.” (V41, 26:42; 38:17)
- **Generalised versus focal:** “A generalised seizure involves networks on both sides of the brain. A focal seizure starts in one area.” (V41, 27:10)
- **Tremor and fear of Parkinson's:** “I know you may be thinking about Parkinson's disease, but there are several possible causes of these symptoms.” (V42, 16:03)
- **Parkinson's:** “It's a long-term condition affecting part of the brain, which can cause difficulties with movement and coordination as well as other symptoms.” (V42, 18:13)
- **Falls support:** “Would you like me to speak with your daughter or son and explain what's happening?” (V42, 32:40)
- **Neuropathy pattern:** “Apart from your hands and feet, is it affecting anywhere else?” / “There are several things that can cause this pattern, so we need to investigate the cause.” (V43, 02:14–02:34)
- **Postural BP:** “I'd like to check how your blood pressure changes when you stand up.” (V43, 05:23)
- **Myasthenia triggers:** “These symptoms can be worse with certain things. Has anything changed recently—for example, how tired you've been?” (V43, 17:56)
- **EMG:** “This test looks at the electrical activity of the muscles.” (V43, 18:36)
