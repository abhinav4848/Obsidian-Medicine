## Diabetes — first consultation
Clarify whether this is new symptoms, an incidental result, poor control, a medicine review or post-admission follow-up. Ask about thirst, polyuria, weight loss, infection, blurred vision and current glucose. Vomiting, abdominal pain, dehydration, drowsiness or rapid/deep breathing raises DKA/HHS concern and needs urgent hospital assessment. New suspected type 1 diabetes requires same-day specialist assessment; age alone cannot distinguish diabetes type.

Explain type 1 as insufficient insulin production and type 2 as insulin resistance with relative deficiency. Explore understanding, blame/fear, work/driving, food access, family support, mood and pregnancy plans. Consider pancreatic disease/cancer where new diabetes accompanies concerning weight loss, abdominal/back symptoms or other red flags.
## Diagnosis and monitoring
Typical diagnostic thresholds: fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, random glucose ≥11.1 with symptoms, two-hour OGTT ≥11.1, or suitable HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol. An asymptomatic abnormal result needs confirmation. Home capillary readings alone do not establish the diagnosis.

HbA1c can mislead with rapid onset/type 1 suspicion, pregnancy, altered red-cell turnover, haemoglobin variants or relevant advanced kidney disease. Use appropriate glucose testing rather than relying on a reassuring HbA1c.

Discuss HbA1c as an approximate longer-term glucose marker, not a day-to-day glucose reading. Agree an individual target considering hypo risk, frailty, comorbidity and treatment burden; commonly 48 with lifestyle/non-hypoglycaemic treatment and 53 with a drug causing hypos in type 2 diabetes. Monitor every 3–6 months until stable, then usually six-monthly. Self-monitoring/CGM is indicated by regimen and hypo risk rather than routinely for everyone.
## Type 1 diabetes and insulin
Ensure specialist team access, structured education such as DAFNE, glucose/ketone monitoring and a written sick-day plan. Basal–bolus injections allow meal adjustment; pumps/CGM and hybrid systems require assessment and training. Do not swap insulin products or doses casually. Review injection technique/sites, storage, timing, meals, exercise and hypo recognition. **Do not stop basal insulin during illness.** Discuss ketone testing and urgent help for vomiting, inability to drink, rising ketones or deterioration.
## Type 2 treatment choices
Check actual medicine use, tolerated doses, HbA1c trend, renal function, weight, CVD/HF, frailty, onset age and preferences before adding treatment. The 2026 NICE pathway emphasises cardiovascular/renal benefit as well as glucose control:
- No relevant comorbidity: offer modified-release metformin plus an SGLT2 inhibitor; sequential initiation can aid tolerability.
- Established atherosclerotic CVD: the pathway also offers subcutaneous semaglutide up to 1 mg weekly.
- CKD eGFR >30: metformin plus suitable SGLT2 inhibitor. At 20–30: dapagliflozin/empagliflozin plus DPP4 inhibitor; do not use metformin below 30. Lower eGFR requires individual options.
- HF, obesity, early-onset disease and frailty have specific branches; use the relevant branch and product restrictions rather than a universal escalation ladder.

Explain benefit, adverse effects, practical administration and review. Offer structured education such as DESMOND, diet/activity support and agreed goals; avoid framing treatment as punishment. [NICE NG28 initial medicines](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng28/chapter/initial-medicines)
## Diabetes medicines — counselling

| Class | Points to cover |
|---|---|
| Metformin | GI effects, gradual titration/MR option, renal function, B12 deficiency risk, sick-day withholding in relevant dehydrating illness |
| Sulfonylurea | Hypoglycaemia, weight gain, meals, renal suitability, driving and safety-critical work |
| DPP4 inhibitor | Low hypo risk alone; dose/renal and liver requirements differ between agents; persistent severe abdominal pain needs assessment |
| SGLT2 inhibitor | Genital infection, dehydration, sick-day rules, ketoacidosis even without very high glucose; urgent assessment for suggestive symptoms |
| GLP1 therapy/tirzepatide | GI effects, pancreatitis warning, product-specific pregnancy/contraception advice and eligibility; avoid assuming all products/doses interchangeable |
| Pioglitazone | Fluid retention/HF and bladder-cancer cautions; discuss weight and fracture risk |
| Insulin | Hypoglycaemia, injection skills, monitoring, sick days, storage and driving |
## Annual review and poorly controlled diabetes
Review HbA1c, BP/lipids/CVD prevention, renal function/urine ACR, foot risk, retinal-screen attendance, smoking, mood, sexual/autonomic symptoms and immunisation needs. Retinal screening interval depends on programme risk, not automatically annual for everyone. Foot assessment includes inspection, pulses and protective sensation; ABPI is not automatically reliable in diabetes.

With poor control, ask about infection, steroids, injection problems, access, adherence and changed circumstances. Work through the patient's priorities, agree treatment/lifestyle steps and set a definite review. A new ulcer, infection, ischaemia or suspected Charcot foot needs the urgent diabetic-foot pathway.
## Hypoglycaemia
Treat glucose <4 mmol/L promptly, even if few symptoms. Establish severity, assistance required, awareness, timing, medicines, meals, exercise/alcohol and recurrence.
- Awake and swallowing safely: fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck after 10–15 minutes and repeat if still low; follow with longer-acting carbohydrate/meal once recovered.
- Unconscious, fitting or unable to swallow: no oral intake; emergency help, airway/recovery-position care and appropriate glucagon/IV glucose by trained staff. Persistent/recurrent or sulfonylurea-related episodes need urgent medical assessment.
- Identify cause, review regimen and establish a prevention plan. Discuss work at heights, machinery, swimming and driving. Use current DVLA group-specific rules, including severe episodes and loss of awareness; do not give one licence rule to everyone.
## Neck lump and thyroid results
Ask onset, progression, pain, exact site, hoarseness, swallowing/breathing, ENT symptoms, constitutional symptoms and exposure history. Airway compromise, severe infection or SVC obstruction needs emergency care. Persistent unexplained or suspicious lumps require urgent appropriate referral; normal TFTs do not exclude thyroid cancer.

| Pattern | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Low TSH, high FT4/FT3 | Thyrotoxicosis; assess cause |
| High TSH, low FT4 | Primary hypothyroidism |
| High TSH, normal FT4 | Subclinical hypothyroidism; confirm persistence/context |
| Discordant results | Review illness, medicines, assay effects and possible pituitary disease |
## Hyperthyroidism
Ask about weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, palpitations/AF, bowel change, menstrual change and eye symptoms; review amiodarone and antithyroid treatment. Graves disease is autoimmune; thyroiditis and toxic nodules differ in treatment. Specialist options include antithyroid drugs, radioiodine and surgery. Pregnancy plans materially affect choices.

**Carbimazole:** baseline FBC/LFTs and thyroid monitoring. Fever, sore throat or mouth ulcers → stop the drug and seek urgent medical assessment/FBC for agranulocytosis. Significant liver symptoms need prompt review. Thyroid storm—marked illness, fever, agitation, tachyarrhythmia/HF—is an emergency. Acute eye pain, diplopia or visual deterioration needs urgent eye assessment.
## Hypothyroidism
Explore fatigue, cold intolerance, constipation, weight/skin/hair change, mood and medicine use. Levothyroxine is usual replacement; explain timing, interactions with iron/calcium and long-term monitoring. Adults <65 without CVD may start around 1.6 micrograms/kg/day; age ≥65 or CVD usually 25–50 micrograms/day with titration. Monitor TSH to stability, then usually annually.

For subclinical disease, consider levothyroxine when TSH ≥10 on two tests three months apart. In symptomatic adults <65 with persistent lesser elevation, consider a six-month trial, then review benefit. Do not treat one borderline result automatically. [NICE NG145](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng145/chapter/recommendations)
## Gynaecomastia
Distinguish diffuse glandular enlargement from a discrete suspicious mass or adiposity. Ask duration, laterality, pain, nipple/skin changes, nodes, puberty, testicular symptoms, liver/thyroid disease and medicines/supplements/anabolic steroids. Examine breasts and relevant endocrine/testicular findings with consent. Explore embarrassment and functional impact. Suspicious features need urgent breast assessment; otherwise treat cause and consider specialist options for persistent troublesome disease.
## Tiredness
Clarify fatigue versus sleepiness, weakness or loss of motivation. Assess sleep quality/apnoea, mood, work pattern, substances, bleeding, infection, malignancy symptoms, cardiopulmonary and endocrine clues. Explain targeted examination and tests rather than “every blood test.” Excessive sleepiness creates driving/work risk. Normal initial tests need review of symptoms and next steps, not dismissal.
## Other endocrine presentations

| Condition | SCA assessment and plan |
|---|---|
| Hyperparathyroidism | Calcium/PTH pattern; renal/bone symptoms, medicines, renal function; specialist assessment |
| Hypoparathyroidism/hypocalcaemia | Tingling, spasms, seizures/arrhythmia; acute symptomatic disease needs hospital treatment |
| Acromegaly | Changed facial/hand/shoe size, sweating, headache/visual symptoms, diabetes; endocrine testing and pituitary imaging pathway |
| Cushing syndrome | Exogenous steroids or endogenous cortisol excess; bruising/striae, proximal weakness, BP/glucose; endocrine assessment. Cushing disease specifically refers to pituitary ACTH disease |
| Primary aldosteronism | Resistant hypertension ± hypokalaemia; specialist aldosterone/renin testing and treatment |
| Phaeochromocytoma | Episodic headache, sweating, palpitations and labile BP; urgent specialist assessment; avoid unsupported treatment changes |
## Adrenal insufficiency
Fatigue, weight loss, hypotension, GI symptoms, pigmentation and autoimmune history can suggest Addison disease. Specialist testing establishes diagnosis; replacement may include glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid. Teach steroid emergency card, sick-day dosing, emergency injection kit/training and never abruptly stopping replacement.

**Suspected adrenal crisis:** vomiting, dehydration, hypotension, severe weakness/confusion or collapse → emergency help and immediate hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV in adults when available, plus resuscitation. Do not wait for tests. Recurrent vomiting preventing absorption needs emergency steroid cover/hospital care. [NICE NG243](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng243/chapter/Recommendations)
## Gout
A hot swollen joint needs consideration of septic arthritis; suspected infection requires immediate referral. Ask flare pattern, tophi, renal disease, diuretics and alcohol/food triggers. Urate may be normal during an attack; repeat after recovery if suspicion remains. Aspiration helps uncertain diagnosis.

Acute treatment: appropriate NSAID (with protection where indicated), colchicine or short oral corticosteroid, selected for comorbidity/interactions. Continue established urate-lowering treatment during a flare. Discuss long-term allopurinol/febuxostat where indicated; allopurinol is preferred with major CVD. Start low, titrate to target with monthly urate and prophylaxis as appropriate. Usually start 2–4 weeks after resolution; frequent flares can justify initiation during a flare. Target <360 micromol/L, consider <300 with tophi/chronic or frequent disease. Explain sustained therapy rather than taking allopurinol only when painful. [NICE NG219](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng219/chapter/Recommendations)
## Example consultation phrases
- **Check both glucose extremes:** “Can I check whether you've been thirstier, passing more urine or losing weight unexpectedly?” / “Have you had symptoms that might mean the sugar is low, such as sweating, feeling shaky or drowsy?” (V19, 06:14–06:49)
- **Understanding:** “What are your thoughts about diabetes?” / “What do you understand by a hypo or an HbA1c?” (V19, 11:01)
- **Explore the meaning of a result:** “What are your thoughts about having a raised HbA1c?” (V19, 11:31)
- **Diabetes:** “It means the level of sugar in the blood is higher than it should be.” (V19, 29:46)
- **Type 1:** “The body's immune system has damaged the cells in the pancreas that normally make insulin, so we need to replace that insulin.” (V19, 15:17)
- **Type 2:** “Your body isn't responding to insulin as efficiently as it should, and over time it may not make enough to meet its needs.” (V19, 01:22; 23:13)
- **Prediabetes:** “Your sugar is higher than normal, but it isn't in the diabetes range. That means a higher risk of developing diabetes, so there are changes we can work on and checks we'll arrange.” (V19, 26:21)
- **Education:** “Has anyone offered you a structured education programme to help with managing diabetes day to day?” (V19, 30:30)
- **Foot care:** “Check your feet regularly, keep them clean, and use shoes that don't rub or break the skin. We can help if nail care is difficult.” (V19, 30:46)
- **Explain a low TSH with an overactive thyroid:** “Think of the brain sending a message to the thyroid. Because the thyroid is already making too much hormone, the brain turns that message down, so the TSH result becomes low.” (V21, 07:47)
- **Underactive thyroid:** “There are several reasons the thyroid can become underactive. Let's work out which is relevant to you.” (V21, 13:10)
- **Parathyroid glands:** “These glands help control the balance of calcium and phosphate in the body.” (V22, 10:53)
- **Cushing terminology:** “Cushing syndrome means the body has too much cortisol. Cushing disease is the particular form caused by a pituitary gland problem.” (V22, 16:51)
- **Synacthen test:** “The test gives a signal that should make the adrenal glands produce more cortisol. The blood tests check whether they respond as expected.” (V22, 22:50)
- **Gout assessment:** “Could I ask a few questions about factors that can contribute to gout?” (V22, 27:54)
