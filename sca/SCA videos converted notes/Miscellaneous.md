## Domestic abuse: recognise and ask safely

Possible cues include injuries with an unclear account, fear of a partner, controlling behaviour, repeated unexplained presentations, self-blame or restricted access to money/healthcare. Abuse can be physical, sexual, emotional, coercive, financial or digital; it is not confined to one gender or relationship type.

**Speak privately**, using an independent interpreter if needed. Avoid asking with the suspected perpetrator present. Explain confidentiality and its limits without promising absolute secrecy.

Use clear, gentle questions: “Do you feel safe at home?” “Has anyone frightened, controlled or hurt you?” Allow the patient to describe events; do not force a disclosure or imply they are responsible.

### Immediate safety assessment

- What happened, when, how often, and is it escalating? Ask about threats to kill, weapons, stalking, sexual violence and **strangulation**.
- Are there injuries, pregnancy, severe distress or suicidal thoughts? Strangulation may cause serious injury without visible marks and needs urgent medical assessment.
- Is the perpetrator nearby? Is it safe to leave today? Where are children or other dependants, and are they also at risk?
- What safe support exists? Can the patient safely receive calls, texts, letters or written information?

Emergency danger or serious injury requires urgent protective/medical action. Do not leave someone at immediate risk with only a routine follow-up appointment.

### Respond and plan

**Validate:** “This is not your fault. You deserve to be safe. We can help you consider your options.” Listen, offer choices and avoid demanding that they leave or confront the perpetrator. Leaving can itself increase risk and needs planning.

Offer specialist domestic abuse advocacy, practical support and a safety plan tailored to the person's circumstances. Discuss a safe destination, trusted contact, essential medicines/documents and how to summon help. Written information or a visible bag can create risk; check what is safe. Arrange a safe means and timing of review. [NHS domestic abuse support](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/getting-help-for-domestic-violence/).

Record the account and findings accurately, including the patient's words where useful. Consider how proxy/online record access could expose sensitive information and follow the local safeguarding process.

## Confidentiality and safeguarding decisions

Seek consent to involve support where possible, but do not promise that an adult's refusal always prevents information sharing. A capacitous adult's wishes usually govern support affecting only them; lawful requirements, serious risk to others and exceptional imminent serious harm can justify or require disclosure.

**Children exposed to domestic abuse need safeguarding consideration**, even if not directly injured. Explain the concern and proposed action when safe, involve the safeguarding lead and follow the local child-protection pathway. Do not promise a particular social-care outcome.

For adults at risk, consider coercion, care/support needs, abuse/neglect and decision-specific capacity. Discuss difficult disclosure decisions promptly with the safeguarding lead and, where needed, seek legal/medicolegal advice. Share only what is necessary and document the risk, consent discussion, reasoning and recipient. [GMC disclosure guidance](https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/the-professional-standards/confidentiality/disclosures-for-the-protection-of-patients-and-others).

## Adult safeguarding and struggling at home

Clarify whether the concern is abuse/exploitation, self-neglect, inability to manage daily tasks, cognitive decline, acute illness or a combination. Do not attribute a sudden deterioration to “old age” or dementia before assessing delirium and physical illness.

Ask about food/fluids, heating, hygiene, falls, continence, mobility, stairs and night-time access to the bathroom. Review medicines, alcohol, sensory impairment, memory, mood and finances. Identify who lives there, who helps, what they actually do and what the patient wants help with. Ask privately where carers may be implicated.

Assess immediate danger, injuries, malnutrition, unsafe medication use and capacity for the particular decision. Organise a coordinated plan: primary care, social care, community nursing, pharmacy, occupational therapy, physiotherapy and appropriate family/carers with consent. Consider home assessment, equipment, benefits advice and a medication review. A compliance aid is useful only after an individual medicines assessment.

Agree a named coordinator and review date rather than offering an unconnected list of services. Residential care is one option to explore when relevant, not a default consequence of asking for support.

## Genetic concerns and counselling

Start with the **question the patient needs answered**: current symptoms, a family diagnosis, predictive testing, reproductive risk or a confirmed result. Clarify the exact diagnosis/variant, who has been tested, relatives affected and age at onset. Obtain family records/results with appropriate consent when possible.

| Inheritance pattern | Useful explanation when the parental genotype is established |
|---|---|
| Autosomal dominant, one heterozygous affected parent | Each child usually has a **50%** chance of inheriting the variant; penetrance/severity can vary |
| Autosomal recessive, both parents carriers | Each pregnancy: **25% affected**, 50% carrier, 25% neither variant |
| X-linked recessive, carrier mother and unaffected father | Each son has a 50% chance of being affected; each daughter a 50% chance of carrying the variant, with condition-specific exceptions |

Examples include Huntington disease, ADPKD and familial hypercholesterolaemia for dominant inheritance; cystic fibrosis and sickle cell disease for recessive inheritance; haemophilia A/B and Duchenne muscular dystrophy for X-linked inheritance. **Do not infer an individual's risk from the disease name alone**: genotype, family relationship, penetrance and unusual inheritance mechanisms matter. [NHS genomics education](https://www.genomicseducation.hee.nhs.uk/genotes/knowledge-hub/autosomal-dominant-inheritance/).

Offer clinical genetics counselling where appropriate. Explain what testing can and cannot answer, possible uncertain results, emotional/family implications, reproductive choices and the right not to know. A predictive result may not establish when or how severely disease will occur. Do not promise that every genetic condition has a test, treatment or inevitable course.

Address current symptoms and distress, relationships, work and future plans. For a concerning result or Huntington-related anxiety, explicitly assess mental health and suicidal thoughts. Specialist discussion should support informed choice rather than push testing.

## Learning disability and communication

Speak directly to the patient, establish preferred language/communication and allow enough time. Use short concrete explanations, accessible materials, visual aids or a trusted supporter with permission. Check understanding with respectful teach-back: “So I know I explained it clearly, can you tell me what the plan is?”

A learning disability **does not imply lack of capacity**. See [GMC capacity guidance](https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/ethical-hub/mental-capacity). Presume capacity, support decision-making and assess only where there is reason for concern. Capacity is specific to the decision and time; consider the ability to understand, retain, use/weigh relevant information and communicate a choice. An unwise decision alone is not incapacity.

Consider private discussion, safeguarding and the patient's own wishes. If capacity is lacking, follow the relevant nation's legal framework, including lawful representatives, best interests/overall benefit and the least restrictive appropriate option. A relative or carer does not automatically have authority to consent.

## Ethical requests: a practical SCA structure

1. **Clarify the request:** who is asking, whose information/decision it concerns, and what authority or consent exists.
2. **Explain the duty simply:** respect the patient's autonomy, confidentiality and safety. A police, employer or family request is not automatically lawful authority to disclose.
3. **Recognise the emotion:** acknowledge frustration or worry without conceding an inappropriate request.
4. **Offer a permissible next step:** obtain consent, arrange direct discussion, provide general information or seek senior/medicolegal advice. You can usually listen to a relative's concern without disclosing the patient's information.
5. **Act on urgency:** do not let an unresolved ethical discussion delay emergency care or necessary safeguarding.

Useful phrasing: “I can hear how worried you are. I can listen to your concerns and use them to help with care. To share their personal information with you, I need their permission or another lawful reason.”
