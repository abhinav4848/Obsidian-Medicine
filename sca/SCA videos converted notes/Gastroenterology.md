## Abdominal symptoms — focused approach
Clarify the patient's meaning of pain, indigestion, diarrhoea or constipation. Establish onset, site/radiation, progression, stool pattern, vomiting, bleeding, jaundice, meals and systemic symptoms. **Cover bowel, urine and reproductive causes** rather than assuming GI disease. Include pregnancy possibility, periods, vaginal/testicular symptoms and relevant surgery.

Review NSAIDs, anticoagulants, steroids, antibiotics, opioids, iron, OTC/herbal drugs and alcohol. Ask about travel/food contacts, IBD/coeliac/cancer family history, toileting access, work, diet, support and fears. Explain why examination, pregnancy testing, urine, DRE or pelvic/groin assessment is needed, with consent/chaperone as appropriate.

**Emergency features:** shock, significant bleeding, peritonism, obstruction, severe dehydration, severe persistent pain, sepsis, acute jaundice with illness, encephalopathy or possible ectopic/AAA/ACS. Do not wait for a precise label before urgent assessment.
## Dyspepsia, reflux and ulcers
Check dysphagia, weight loss, bleeding/melaena, persistent vomiting, anaemia, mass, drug triggers and possible cardiac pain. Tailor food/meal timing, weight and smoking/alcohol advice to actual triggers rather than banning everything.

Uncomplicated uninvestigated dyspepsia: full-dose PPI for four weeks or H. pylori test-and-treat as appropriate; review response. Breath/stool antigen testing needs PPI washout of two weeks and usually no antibiotics for four weeks. If positive, use current local eradication regimen accounting for allergy and previous antibiotic exposure; explain adherence and adverse effects.

GORD can be diagnosed clinically; endoscopy is not mandatory for every reflux presentation. PPI usually 4–8 weeks, then lowest effective strategy; severe oesophagitis/other indications may require long-term therapy. Reassess persistent/refractory symptoms rather than repeatedly issuing prescriptions.

Peptic ulcer: address NSAIDs, H. pylori and bleeding risk. Gastric ulcer may require repeat endoscopy at 6–8 weeks to confirm healing/exclude malignancy; follow the endoscopy plan. Eradication confirmation is particularly relevant in ulcer disease. Functional dyspepsia is real symptoms without explanatory structural disease; explain treatment and review rather than “nothing is wrong.” [NICE CG184](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg184/chapter/Recommendations)
## Barrett oesophagus and hiatus hernia
Explain Barrett as a change in the lower oesophageal lining associated with reflux and increased cancer risk; it is not itself a diagnosis of cancer. Specialist surveillance/treatment depends on segment and dysplasia. Hiatus hernia means stomach protrusion through the diaphragm; treat reflux and assess complications/need for specialist input. Do not imply every hernia needs surgery.
## Upper GI cancer prompts
Dysphagia warrants urgent suspected-cancer assessment. Age ≥55 with weight loss plus upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia is another important trigger. An upper abdominal mass, unexplained jaundice or concerning new diabetes/weight loss may require a specific pancreatic/hepatobiliary pathway. Explain what is suspected, investigation urgency and how to chase the appointment; avoid false reassurance from a PPI response.
## Abnormal liver tests and jaundice
Review trend, pattern and degree alongside symptoms and prior results. ALT/AST suggest hepatocellular injury; ALP/GGT suggest cholestasis; isolated bilirubin needs conjugated/unconjugated context. Albumin/clotting help assess synthetic function. A single marker is not a diagnosis; ALP can be bone derived.

Ask about alcohol honestly, obesity/metabolic disease, medicines/overdose, travel, sexual/blood exposure, tattoos, injecting drugs and family history. Look for jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, ascites, bleeding and encephalopathy. Select aetiology testing (viral, autoimmune, iron studies and other inherited disease) and ultrasound according to presentation.

Acute jaundice with pain/fever, confusion, bleeding/coagulopathy, renal dysfunction or possible overdose → urgent hospital assessment. Painless jaundice can signal obstruction/malignancy. Do not use a bilirubin number alone to decide safety. Gilbert syndrome is benign intermittent unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia after excluding relevant alternatives; it does not cause impaired liver function.
## Fatty liver, cirrhosis and ascites
Metabolic fatty liver often accompanies obesity/diabetes and CVD risk. Assess fibrosis risk through the appropriate pathway even if transaminases are modest; ultrasound alone does not stage fibrosis. Agree sustainable weight/activity/diet changes and manage metabolic risks. Suspected advanced fibrosis or diagnostic uncertainty requires specialist review.

Explain cirrhosis as scarring, with compensated versus decompensated disease. New ascites, GI bleeding, encephalopathy or infection needs urgent assessment. Specialist care may include portal-hypertension treatment, surveillance, diuretics/paracentesis and transplant assessment. Abdominal swelling also has malignant, cardiac and renal causes. Avoid promising reversibility or a transplant.
## Viral hepatitis

| Infection | Key counselling |
|---|---|
| Hepatitis A | Usually faecal–oral and self-limiting; hydration/symptom care, liver monitoring and public-health/contact advice; assess severity |
| Hepatitis B | Blood/sexual/perinatal transmission; specialist assessment of acute/chronic infection, contact testing/vaccination and prevention |
| Hepatitis C | Antibody indicates exposure; RNA establishes current infection. Refer for modern curative antiviral treatment; prevention and reinfection advice |
HBsAg suggests current HBV infection; anti-HBc indicates exposure and may persist in current or past infection, so interpret with the complete panel. Testing after a recent exposure depends on window period and assay. Do not delay time-sensitive post-exposure advice while awaiting routine repeat tests.
## Diarrhoea
Define frequency, consistency, blood/mucus, urgency, nocturnal symptoms, duration and baseline. Assess hydration, antibiotics/C. difficile risk, travel, food contacts, medicines and immunosuppression. Consider infection, IBD, coeliac disease, cancer, IBS, overflow and malabsorption/endocrine disease.

Give oral rehydration and practical infection-control/work advice; food-handling/healthcare roles may have additional requirements. Antimotility treatment is not suitable for every diarrhoea, particularly bloody/systemic illness. Persistent symptoms need targeted blood/stool tests and follow-up. Severe bloody diarrhoea, toxicity or dehydration merits urgent assessment.
## Constipation and laxatives
Ask what “constipation” means: hard stool, infrequency, straining or incomplete evacuation. Establish last stool/flatus, overflow, pain/vomiting/distension, bleeding, weight loss, diet/fluid, activity, painful anal disease and medicines. Consider metabolic, neurological and pelvic causes.

Exclude obstruction before routine laxatives. Address cause, toileting, appropriate fibre/fluid and activity. Choose bulk-forming, osmotic or stimulant treatment by stool pattern, comorbidity and response; explain expected delay and GI effects. Impaction needs a specific disimpaction plan rather than generic fibre advice. Persistent new change or cancer features needs investigation.
## IBS
A positive diagnosis can follow an appropriate symptom pattern and assessment; it is not endless exclusion of all disease. Look for pain associated with defaecation/stool change, bloating, meal relationship and chronicity. Check red flags and appropriate FBC, inflammatory markers and coeliac serology; faecal calprotectin can help when IBD is a concern and cancer is not suspected.

Explain a sensitive gut–brain interaction, with symptoms that are real and manageable. Agree regular meals, individual trigger changes, soluble rather than indiscriminate insoluble fibre, activity and stress support. Dietitian-guided low-FODMAP may help refractory symptoms. Consider antispasmodic for pain, suitable laxative for constipation, loperamide for diarrhoea; selected low-dose TCA/psychological therapy for persistent symptoms. New red flags require reassessment despite a previous IBS label.
## Coeliac disease
Consider GI symptoms, iron/folate deficiency, fatigue, growth problems, autoimmune disease or family history. Test **total IgA and IgA tTG**; use IgA EMA for weak positivity and IgG-based tests when IgA deficient. The patient must be eating gluten for accurate testing; do not start a gluten-free diet before the diagnostic pathway is complete. Positive tests or high suspicion need appropriate specialist assessment, with biopsy/no-biopsy protocols depending on context.

After confirmation: lifelong gluten-free diet, dietitian, nutritional/bone assessment, relevant vaccinations and review of persistent symptoms. [NICE NG20](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng20/chapter/Recommendations)
## Rectal bleeding and colorectal cancer
Ask amount/colour, mixed versus surface blood, pain, stool change, tenesmus, weight loss and anaemia symptoms. Haemorrhoids or anticoagulants do not exclude cancer. Examine appropriately and use symptomatic FIT under the current pathway. FIT ≥10 micrograms Hb/g stool supports urgent suspected-cancer referral; rectal mass warrants referral without waiting for FIT. A negative/unreturned FIT must not delay referral where concern remains.

Explain colonoscopy/CT colonography and possible biopsy without promising the result. Familial polyposis or Lynch-type family histories need genetics/surveillance assessment; family implications may be the main consultation issue.
## Inflammatory bowel disease
Crohn disease can affect any GI site with skip/transmural disease; UC affects the colon from the rectum. Ask about bloody/nocturnal diarrhoea, weight loss, perianal disease and extraintestinal eye, joint, skin or oral symptoms. New suspected disease needs gastroenterology assessment; severe flare with systemic illness, frequent bloody stool, severe pain or dehydration needs urgent hospital review.

For established disease, clarify flare plan, adherence, infection and immunosuppressant monitoring. Steroids induce remission in selected contexts, not long-term maintenance. Aminosalicylates, immunomodulators, biologics and surgery depend on disease and specialist plan. Discuss nutrition, employment, toilet access, psychological support and cancer surveillance.
## Anal conditions
- **Haemorrhoids:** bleeding/prolapse/itch; assess other causes. Grade I no prolapse, II self-reducing, III manual reduction, IV irreducible. Stool optimisation and short symptom treatment; persistent prolapse/bleeding may need procedures. Acute severe pain suggests thrombosis/strangulation or another diagnosis.
- **Fissure:** pain with/after stool ± bright blood. Stool softening, analgesia, appropriate local therapy; persistent disease may need GTN/diltiazem or specialist treatment. Atypical lesions need reassessment.
- **Pruritus ani:** assess dermatological, anal, infectious and irritant causes. Gentle hygiene, avoid scratching/irritants and treat cause rather than repeated empirical steroids.
## Gallstones and pancreas
Biliary colic: episodic upper abdominal pain ± radiation/meal association; ultrasound/LFTs and elective surgical discussion where symptomatic. Persistent pain, fever, jaundice or sepsis suggests cholecystitis/cholangitis/obstruction and needs urgent assessment.

Acute pancreatitis: severe epigastric/back pain, vomiting and systemic illness → admission; assess gallstones, alcohol, medicines and metabolic causes. Chronic pancreatitis can cause pain, steatorrhoea, weight loss and diabetes; manage nutrition, enzyme replacement and diabetes with specialists.

Pancreatic cancer may present with painless jaundice, weight loss, abdominal/back pain or new diabetes. Use urgent investigation/referral criteria. Do not quote population survival as an individual prognosis.
## Appendicitis and hernias
Appendicitis may migrate from central to right lower abdominal pain, but children/pregnancy can be atypical. Consider urinary and gynaecological alternatives including ectopic pregnancy. Suspected acute disease needs same-day surgical assessment; do not arrange routine outpatient imaging first.

A hernia is tissue protruding through a weakness. Assess pain, reducibility, vomiting, distension and obstruction. An acutely painful irreducible hernia or strangulation signs is an emergency. Femoral hernias have particular complication risk and need surgical assessment. Explain elective options and activity/work implications without promising technique or timing.
## Weight loss and obesity
Unintentional loss: verify trend, appetite/intake and whether the claimed intentional loss fits the scale. Screen for malignancy, infection, endocrine disease, malabsorption, mood/eating disorder and food insecurity. Select examination/tests and a dated review; avoid a broad scan request without a reason.

For weight management ask permission, explore prior attempts and goals, assess comorbidity/medicines, eating pattern, activity, sleep and barriers. BMI thresholds vary by ethnicity and clinical context. Agree practical changes and support, with medicines or bariatric assessment when eligible. Options include orlistat and injectable treatments; eligibility, commissioning and stopping rules differ. Discuss pregnancy/contraception and adverse effects; tirzepatide requires additional contraceptive precautions around initiation/escalation. [NICE NG246](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng246/chapter/Medicines-and-surgery)

## Example consultation phrases
- **Localise symptoms:** “You mentioned pain in your tummy. Can you show me roughly where you're getting it?”
- **Diet:** “Could you describe a typical day of eating? Do you avoid any foods, or have any intolerances or dietary restrictions?”
- **Relevant habits:** “Some habits can trigger indigestion or bowel symptoms, including smoking and alcohol. Could I ask about those?”
- **Medicines:** “Can I check whether you're taking ibuprofen or other anti-inflammatory painkillers?”
- **Practical reflux changes:** “There are some simple changes that can help. From what you've told me, we could look at your trigger foods and the size of meals.”
- **Initial options:** “There are two initial approaches we can discuss: a trial of acid-reducing medicine, or testing for the stomach bacterium H. pylori and treating it if needed.” Use only where the clinical pathway supports these choices.
- **H. pylori:** “It's a bacterium that can live in the stomach and contribute to symptoms or ulcers.”
- **Hiatus hernia:** “Part of the stomach has moved up through the opening in the diaphragm into the chest. This can contribute to reflux symptoms.”
- **Jaundice:** “The yellow colour comes from a substance called bilirubin building up. We need to find out why that is happening.”
- **Diarrhoea:** “What do you mean by diarrhoea—is the stool loose or watery, are you going more often, or both?”
- **Constipation:** “What does constipation mean for you—hard stool, difficulty passing it, going less often, or something else?”
- **IBS:** “The bowel is more sensitive or irritable than usual. That can produce several symptoms, and certain triggers can make them worse.”
- **Bleeding concern:** “I can see that noticing the blood was a shock and made you worried.” Follow with their specific concern rather than assuming it is cancer.
- **Crohn's and skip lesions:** “Inflammation can affect different parts of the gut, with stretches of normal bowel between affected areas. That's what 'skip lesions' means.”
- **Anal fissure:** “It's a small tear in the lining of the back passage. Hard stool and straining can contribute, and the tear can cause pain and bleeding.”
- **Appendicitis:** “The appendix has become inflamed. One concern is that it could burst, which is why suspected appendicitis needs urgent hospital assessment.”
