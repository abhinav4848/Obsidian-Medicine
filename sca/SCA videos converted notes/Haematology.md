## Anaemia — assess severity and cause
Review Hb trend, MCV, other cell lines, symptoms and clinical stability. Ask about dyspnoea, chest pain, syncope, bleeding, GI symptoms, periods/pregnancy, diet, medicines, renal/inflammatory disease and haemoglobinopathy family history. Severe symptoms, active bleeding, rapid decline or instability need urgent assessment; urgency is not decided by Hb alone.

Explain reduced oxygen-carrying capacity in plain language. Establish fatigue/function, diet affordability, beliefs about iron and concern about cancer. Arrange appropriate examination, ferritin/iron studies, reticulocytes, B12/folate, renal/liver/inflammatory tests and cause-directed investigations. Mixed deficiencies can produce a normal MCV.

| Pattern | Important possibilities |
|---|---|
| Microcytic | Iron deficiency, thalassaemia, some chronic inflammatory disease |
| Macrocytic | B12/folate deficiency, alcohol/liver disease, medicines, marrow disease |
| Normocytic | Acute blood loss, renal/inflammatory disease, haemolysis or mixed causes |
## Iron deficiency
Identify **why**: menstrual/GI/urinary loss, diet, malabsorption, pregnancy or other pathology. Ferritin can be falsely normal/high during inflammation. Do not assume piles or menstruation explain every case; use age, symptoms and appropriate GI/cancer pathways.

Discuss oral iron, GI effects, stool darkening, interactions and alternatives if poorly tolerated. Monitor Hb response and replenish stores after correction; persistent failure needs adherence/cause review and possible IV iron/specialist input. Dietary iron complements replacement, not a substitute for investigating unexplained anaemia.
## B12 and folate
Ask about neurological symptoms (sensory change, gait, weakness, cognition), glossitis, diet, gastric/ileal surgery, metformin/acid suppression and nitrous oxide. B12 deficiency can exist without macrocytosis or anaemia. Autoimmune gastritis and malabsorption affect treatment duration/route.

Take diagnostic bloods before replacement where possible, but **do not delay treatment for suspected megaloblastic disease with neurological symptoms**. Seek urgent specialist advice for neurological involvement. Lifelong IM B12 is indicated for autoimmune gastritis, total gastrectomy or complete terminal ileal resection; oral/IM options in other causes depend on absorption, severity and preference. Review symptoms rather than repeatedly checking B12 during IM treatment. Replace B12 before or alongside folate when both deficient; never give folate alone with untreated B12 deficiency. [NICE NG239](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng239/chapter/recommendations)
## Haemoglobinopathies
**Thalassaemia:** inherited globin-chain disorder, ranging from asymptomatic trait to severe disease. Microcytosis does not automatically mean iron deficiency; check iron before prescribing. Specialist haemoglobin testing/genetic counselling clarifies diagnosis and reproductive risk. Transfusion and chelation are for selected disease, not every carrier.

**Sickle cell:** establish phenotype, specialist plan and previous crises. Acute severe pain, fever, chest symptoms, hypoxia, neurological change or priapism needs urgent assessment. Discuss hydration/trigger avoidance, immunisations, infection prevention, medicines and family planning with the specialist team. Do not assume trait and disease have the same risk.
## Spleen and abnormal white cells
Splenomegaly can reflect haematological malignancy, infection, haemolysis, liver/portal disease or inflammatory disease. Examine and investigate the context; pain/trauma or severe illness changes urgency.

After splenectomy/functional asplenia, ensure immunisation and antibiotic-prevention plan, emergency infection advice and medical alert information. Fever can become serious quickly. Address travel/malaria and animal-bite risk; schedules depend on current national/local guidance.

Interpret differential white counts with trend, film and clinical context. Neutropenia plus fever/illness, particularly after chemotherapy or marrow-suppressing drugs, is a medical emergency. Do not simply book a repeat FBC. Consider drug causes including carbimazole; eosinophilia can reflect atopy, parasites or other disease.
## Bruising and bleeding
Ask spontaneous/traumatic pattern, mucosal/urine/GI/menstrual bleeding, dental/surgical history, family history and antiplatelet/anticoagulant/NSAID use. Check systemic symptoms and safeguarding, including non-mobile infants or vulnerable adults. Significant bleeding, sepsis, neurological symptoms or suspected abuse requires urgent action.

Initial tests commonly include FBC/film, clotting and renal/liver function; normal clotting tests do not exclude every bleeding disorder. Thrombocytopenia may be drug, immune, marrow, infection or systemic disease; assess count, bleeding and trend. Reactive thrombocytosis can accompany inflammation/iron deficiency, but persistent unexplained elevation needs assessment.

Inherited examples: von Willebrand disease (mucocutaneous bleeding; inheritance varies by type), haemophilia A (factor VIII) and B (factor IX). Specialist treatment and procedural planning are individual; do not prescribe factor regimens from a brief history.
## Thrombophilia and anticoagulation
Treat suspected acute VTE through the urgent pathway; see [[CVS#DVT and swollen legs]]. Thrombophilia testing is selective and timing/anticoagulants can affect results. Recurrent/unusual events, family history or antiphospholipid syndrome may warrant haematology input.

**Warfarin:** explain INR, individual target/monitoring, interactions, consistent diet, alcohol, pregnancy implications and bleeding precautions. Loading/reversal is protocol-specific, not a universal 10 mg start. **DOACs:** no routine INR, but baseline and periodic FBC, liver/renal function, weight, interactions and adherence review are required; renal dosing uses appropriate creatinine clearance. Monitoring is usually at least annual and more frequent with frailty/renal impairment. [NHS SPS](https://www.sps.nhs.uk/monitorings/doacs-direct-oral-anticoagulants-monitoring/)

For any anticoagulant, provide alert card, missed-dose instructions specific to the product, bleeding/head-injury advice and procedure/dental contact guidance. DOACs are not suitable for every valve or thrombophilia indication.
## Suspected blood cancer
- **Leukaemia:** unexplained pallor/fatigue, infections/fever, bruising/bleeding, bone pain, nodes or hepatosplenomegaly → very urgent FBC under cancer guidance, with immediate hospital assessment if acutely unwell. Abnormal results suggesting acute leukaemia need urgent discussion.
- **Lymphoma:** unexplained lymphadenopathy/splenomegaly with or without fever, sweats, weight loss or itch → appropriate suspected-cancer pathway. A biopsy determines type; do not reassure solely because nodes are painless.
- **Myeloma:** persistent bone/back pain or unexplained fracture, anaemia, renal impairment or hypercalcaemia → FBC, calcium, inflammatory markers and appropriate paraprotein/free-light-chain testing. Cord compression, severe hypercalcaemia or acute renal injury is an emergency. MGUS needs monitoring but is not itself myeloma.

For known cancer, establish the plan, current toxicity/infection risks, understanding and practical/emotional needs. Explain tests or treatment at the appropriate level and acknowledge uncertainty about prognosis.
## Example consultation phrases
- **Introduce anaemia safety questions:** “When the haemoglobin is low, there are a few questions I need to ask to check how it's affecting you.” Then ask explicitly about chest pain, breathlessness, palpitations and faintness.
- **Find the cause:** “Let me ask a few questions about possible causes so we can understand why this is happening.”
- **Explain low iron:** “There isn't enough iron available to make the red blood cells normally.” Link replacement to investigating the underlying cause.
- **Blood-results transition:** “Let's go through some of your blood test results.” Explain the actual Hb, cell size and iron results in plain language.
- **Why examine the abdomen:** “The blood result tells us there is anaemia, but we also need to look for its cause. Examining your tummy may give us important clues.”
- **B12 symptoms:** “Have you noticed any soreness or changes with your tongue?” / “Have you had any problems with your vision?” Also ask about neurological symptoms.
- **Dietary B12:** “Foods such as fish, meat, eggs, dairy and fortified cereals can provide B12.” Tailor to diet and cause; food alone may not treat a deficiency.
- **Dietary folate:** “Green leafy vegetables, lentils, chickpeas and citrus fruit can help increase folate in the diet.”
- **Spleen:** “The spleen helps remove old blood cells and helps the body fight certain infections. If it isn't working, we need an extra infection-prevention plan.”
- **Warfarin monitoring:** “We use a blood test called the INR to guide the dose. We check more frequently while finding the right dose, and may space the tests out when it is stable.”
- **Bleeding worry:** “There are treatments that can reverse warfarin when necessary, but any significant bleeding still needs urgent assessment.”
- **Leukaemia:** “Abnormal blood-forming cells build up in the bone marrow and can affect the production of healthy blood cells.”
- **Myeloma:** “It affects plasma cells, which are part of the immune system. Abnormal proteins made by these cells can be found in blood or urine tests.”
- **MGUS and cancer worry:** “The tests have found an abnormal protein made by some plasma cells. MGUS is not cancer, and most people remain well, but monitoring is important because it can occasionally change into another condition.” [Myeloma UK](https://www.myeloma.org.uk/library/monoclonal-gammopathy-of-undetermined-significance-mgus-infosheet/)
- **Haematology clinic:** “They may recommend more blood tests, scans or a sample from the bone marrow, depending on what the results suggest.”
