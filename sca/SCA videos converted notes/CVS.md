## Consultation framework
- Establish where you are joining the story: new symptom, results, treatment review or post-discharge counselling. Do not retake an entire established history.
- **Safety first:** current chest pain, breathlessness at rest, syncope, shock, focal neurological symptoms or a cold painful limb change the consultation immediately.
- Focus PMH on CVD, diabetes, renal disease, arrhythmias and relevant inherited disease. Ask about premature CVD and sudden unexplained death in relatives.
- Reconcile actual medicine use, adverse effects, OTC NSAIDs, steroids, stimulants and anticoagulants.
- Explore functional change: walking distance, stairs, sleep, work/driving, caring duties and support at home. Link smoking, alcohol, diet and activity questions to this presentation.
- Ask what the patient understands, fears and hopes to achieve; address those answers in the plan.
## Primary prevention
**Explain:** “We estimate your chance of a future heart attack or stroke so we can decide together how to reduce it.” Describe atherosclerosis as fatty deposits narrowing arteries.

Use QRISK3 in adults aged 25–84 without established CVD, including type 2 diabetes. Type 1 diabetes, familial lipid disorders and CKD with eGFR <60 and/or albuminuria have separate high-risk pathways. A family history of ordinary high cholesterol alone is not familial hypercholesterolaemia.

| Situation | Prevention discussion |
|---|---|
| QRISK3 ≥10% | Offer atorvastatin 20 mg daily |
| QRISK3 <10% | Address lifestyle; a statin can still suit informed preference or underestimated risk |
| Age ≥85 | Consider atorvastatin 20 mg, with frailty, prognosis and treatment burden in mind |
| Type 1 diabetes | Offer if >40, duration >10 years, nephropathy or other CVD risk factors; consider in younger adults |
| CKD | Offer atorvastatin 20 mg; titration depends on renal function |
Explain percentages as natural frequencies over a stated period: “About 20 of 100 similar people over ten years,” without suggesting certainty. Discuss absolute benefit and harms; do not invent an individual treatment benefit. Aspirin is not routine primary prevention. Agree achievable changes and a review rather than delivering a generic lifestyle list. [NICE NG238](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng238/chapter/Recommendations)
## Chest pain and suspected ACS
### Focused assessment
Ask early: **“Do you have pain now?” “When exactly was the last episode?” “Has it happened at rest?”** Establish onset, duration, radiation, exertional pattern, progression and accompanying sweating, nausea, breathlessness or collapse.

| Pattern | Consider |
|---|---|
| Pressure/tightness, exertional, radiates to arm/jaw | Ischaemia; worsening/rest pain raises ACS concern |
| Sudden severe pain into back, pulse/BP asymmetry | Aortic dissection |
| Pleuritic pain with dyspnoea/haemoptysis | PE or other respiratory disease |
| Sharp pain worse supine, relieved leaning forward | Pericarditis |
| Burning with reflux/meal or posture association | Gastro-oesophageal disease |
| Local tenderness/movement association | Musculoskeletal disease |
| Episodes with panic/hyperventilation | Anxiety may contribute; do not use it to exclude cardiac disease |
### Suspected ACS: decide urgency
- **Emergency transfer:** ongoing pain; or pain within 12 hours with abnormal/unavailable ECG. Do not delay transfer to obtain a trace.
- **Urgent same-day hospital assessment:** now pain-free, pain within 12 hours with normal ECG, or last pain 12–72 hours ago.
- **More than 72 hours:** detailed assessment, ECG and troponin with timing-aware interpretation; referral urgency follows clinical judgement. This is not an automatic routine chest-pain-clinic pathway.
- Pulmonary oedema or recurrent pain after suspected ACS requires urgent reassessment. A normal ECG does not exclude ACS. [NICE CG95](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg95/chapter/recommendations)

For suspected ACS arrange help promptly, give aspirin 300 mg unless contraindicated, and consider GTN for pain if appropriate. Oxygen is for hypoxaemia, not routine use; target range depends on hypercapnic respiratory-failure risk. Explain the concern and hospital assessment. Arrange safe transport, support for dependants and permission to contact family. The patient must not drive themself.
## Post-MI review
- Check current pain, dyspnoea, oedema, palpitations or syncope before a routine review.
- Establish discharge date, intervention, LV function, discharge plan and what remains unclear. Ask about fear of another event, mood, sex, employment, driving and family concerns.
- Reconcile **ACE inhibitor/ARB, statin, beta-blocker and antiplatelet regimen** against the discharge plan. Duration and choice depend on LV function, intervention, bleeding risk and other indications; do not promise every drug is lifelong.
- Check renal/potassium monitoring after ACE inhibitor initiation/titration. Explain why dual antiplatelets must not be stopped casually, particularly after a stent; seek cardiology advice for bleeding or planned procedures.
- Offer cardiac rehabilitation: graded exercise, education and psychological support. Build activity towards an appropriate target through rehabilitation; do not prescribe sudden strenuous exercise after discharge.
- Address smoking, diet, weight and alcohol sensitively; adult low-risk alcohol guidance is no more than 14 units/week, spread across days.
- Give an explicit recurrence plan and arrange follow-up. Driving advice must match licence group, treatment and complications: use current DVLA rules rather than a universal interval.
## Stable angina
Confirm the predictable exertional pattern and exclude new rest pain, crescendo symptoms or ongoing pain. Ask about activity restriction, GTN use, medication adherence and fear of exercise.

**Explain:** “The heart muscle is temporarily getting less oxygen than it needs, usually because its arteries have narrowed.”

- Short-acting GTN for episodes and before predictable exertion. Sit down; warn about headache/dizziness and check PDE5-inhibitor use. Repeat after five minutes if pain persists; if still present five minutes after the second dose, call 999. A changed/severe pattern needs earlier emergency assessment.
- First maintenance choice is a beta-blocker or CCB. Review symptoms, pulse/BP, contraindications and tolerability before titration, switching or combination therapy. When combining with a beta-blocker use a dihydropyridine such as amlodipine, not verapamil.
- Persistent symptoms or intolerance: discuss cardiology options including long-acting nitrate, ivabradine, nicorandil or ranolazine; selection is individual.
- Secondary prevention: consider aspirin 75 mg, high-intensity statin, risk-factor treatment and ACE inhibitor where indicated.
- Explain PCI as widening a narrowed artery, usually with a stent; CABG creates an alternative route for blood flow. Do not promise a procedure based only on the number of narrowed vessels.
## Palpitations
Ask what the patient means; invite them to tap the rhythm. Establish sudden/gradual onset, regularity, duration, frequency, termination, exertion and associated symptoms. Review caffeine/energy drinks, alcohol, recreational drugs, inhalers, QT-prolonging medicines, anaemia/thyroid symptoms and sudden-death family history.

**High risk:** syncope, exertional episodes, chest pain, significant breathlessness, haemodynamic compromise or concerning inherited/structural disease. Arrange urgent assessment appropriate to severity.

Request a 12-lead ECG and targeted bloods. Intermittent symptoms may need ambulatory monitoring chosen for episode frequency. Echo is for suspected structural disease, not every palpitation. Explain that anxiety and arrhythmia can coexist. Explore work at heights, machinery and driving if incapacitation is possible.
## AF and atrial flutter
Confirm AF with ECG; intermittent suspected AF may require ambulatory recording. Identify duration, stability, precipitant and heart failure. New neurological deficits, shock, ischaemic pain or pulmonary oedema need emergency care.

**Explain three aims:** control symptoms/rate, consider rhythm restoration where appropriate, and prevent stroke.

- Rate control commonly uses a standard beta-blocker or rate-limiting CCB, selected for comorbidity. Avoid verapamil/diltiazem in reduced-EF heart failure. Digoxin monotherapy suits selected non-paroxysmal AF with little exercise or unsuitable alternatives.
- Inadequate control needs review and sometimes combination treatment/specialist advice. Never interpret “combine two drugs” as permission to combine a beta-blocker with verapamil routinely.
- Rhythm control/cardioversion is a specialist decision based on onset, symptoms, causes, heart failure and anticoagulation. “Pill in the pocket” requires specialist selection and instruction.
- **Stroke prevention:** assess CHA2DS2-VASc and ORBIT. Offer DOAC at score ≥2; consider for men scoring 1. Sex alone does not justify treatment. Address modifiable bleeding risks; a high bleeding score or falls is not automatically a reason to withhold anticoagulation.
- Warfarin is used when DOACs are unsuitable and for specific indications. Check renal function, interactions, dosing, adherence and bleeding advice. AF becoming undetectable does not itself justify stopping anticoagulation.
- Atrial flutter needs specialist rhythm/ablation discussion and stroke-risk assessment, not simply an identical rate-control prescription. [NICE NG196](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng196/chapter/recommendations)
## Heart failure
### Suspected diagnosis
Ask about trajectory, exertional capacity, orthopnoea, PND, oedema, weight change, chest pain, palpitations and precipitants such as NSAIDs. Explore sleep, ADLs, carers and fear of the word “failure.”

**Explain:** “The heart is not pumping or filling as effectively as it should. It does not mean it has stopped.” Explain an echo as ultrasound assessing the heart's structure and function.

Severe acute dyspnoea, hypoxia, pulmonary oedema, ACS features or shock needs emergency hospital care. Do not wait for community BNP testing.

For stable suspected chronic HF obtain NT-proBNP plus ECG and relevant bloods:
- >2,000 ng/L: specialist assessment and echo within two weeks.
- 400–2,000: within six weeks.
- <400 in an untreated person: HF less likely; review alternatives, but discuss persistent clinical suspicion. Obesity and treatment can lower levels; renal/systemic illness can raise them.
### Established diagnosis
Clarify echo phenotype, fluid status, cause and specialist plan. Diuretics relieve congestion; disease-modifying therapy depends on EF. Reduced EF: offer ACE inhibitor, evidence-based beta-blocker, MRA and SGLT2 inhibitor, introduced and monitored individually. Persistent symptoms on tolerated therapy may justify ACE-to-ARNI switch with specialist input. Mildly reduced EF: consider these classes; preserved EF: consider MRA/SGLT2 treatment. [NICE NG106](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng106/chapter/Recommendations)

Provide renal/electrolyte monitoring, weight/symptom guidance, rehabilitation and HF nurse access. Avoid blanket fluid/salt restriction; individualise. Refractory disease may need device/revascularisation assessment. Advanced disease warrants shared goals, symptom control, carer support and palliative care alongside cardiology; prognosis cannot be reduced to a fixed countdown.
## Hypertension
### Confirm and assess
Check measurement technique, repeated readings, both arms initially and standing BP when indicated. Ask about medicines, adherence, pregnancy possibility, renal/endocrine disease and symptoms of organ injury.

| Stage | Clinic BP | Daytime ABPM/HBPM average |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 140/90–159/99 | 135/85–149/94 |
| 2 | ≥160/100 but below severe range | ≥150/95 |
| Severe | Systolic ≥180 OR diastolic ≥120 | Assess urgently; do not await routine monitoring if organ injury suspected |
HBPM: two seated measurements at least one minute apart, morning and evening for 4–7 days; discard day 1 and average the remainder. ABPM: at least two daytime readings/hour and average at least 14.

Assess urine ACR/haematuria, renal function/electrolytes, HbA1c, lipids, fundoscopy and ECG. Severe BP with retinal haemorrhage/papilloedema, confusion, chest pain, HF or AKI needs same-day specialist assessment. Severe asymptomatic BP still needs prompt organ-damage assessment and review, not reassurance alone.
### Treatment conversation
Persistent stage 2 usually warrants treatment; discuss stage 1 treatment with adults <80 who have organ damage, CVD, renal disease, diabetes or ten-year risk ≥10%. Consider younger adults with lower calculated risk; account for lifetime risk. Age ≥80 and frailty require individual decisions.

Step 1: type 2 diabetes → ACE inhibitor/ARB; otherwise <55 and not Black African/African-Caribbean family origin → ACE inhibitor/ARB; age ≥55 or Black African/African-Caribbean family origin without type 2 diabetes → CCB. Prefer ARB when renin–angiotensin therapy is selected in Black African/African-Caribbean family origin. If CCB unsuitable consider a thiazide-like diuretic.

Step 2: combine suitable classes. Step 3: ACE inhibitor OR ARB + CCB + thiazide-like diuretic. Before escalation review adherence and tolerated doses; never combine ACE inhibitor with ARB. Resistant hypertension requires confirmation, potassium/renal review and fourth-drug or specialist discussion.

Typical clinic targets: <140/90 under 80; <150/90 at 80+. Home/daytime targets: <135/85 and <145/85 respectively. Individualise with frailty, postural symptoms and comorbid pathways. [NICE NG136](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng136/chapter/recommendations)
## Cardiomyopathy, pericarditis and endocarditis
- **Cardiomyopathy:** clarify phenotype, symptoms and family history. Dilated = impaired contraction; hypertrophic = thickened muscle, sometimes obstruction; restrictive = impaired filling. Exertional syncope and sudden-death family history need urgent specialist consideration. Discuss inherited-cardiac assessment and individual exercise advice; do not equate all hypertrophic disease with obstruction.
- **Pericarditis:** pleuritic/postural pain may follow infection but can mimic ACS. Acute suspected cases need urgent assessment. Hospital work-up may include ECG, inflammatory markers and echo. Tamponade is impaired filling from pressure around the heart: hypotension, raised JVP and muffled sounds are classic clues, not a required complete triad.
- **Endocarditis:** fever plus new/changing murmur, prosthetic valve or other risk factors warrants urgent hospital assessment. Explain infection of the heart lining/valve; cultures and echo guide prolonged antibiotics and sometimes surgery. Do not wait for peripheral stigmata before referring.
## PAD and limb ischaemia
Characterise exertional calf/thigh/buttock pain, reproducible walking distance and relief with rest. Compare neurogenic claudication (posture related), neuropathy and MSK causes. Examine skin, ulcers and pulses; ABPI supports assessment, but a normal/high value in diabetes does not exclude PAD.

**Acute limb ischaemia:** sudden pain, pallor, absent pulses, paraesthesia, paralysis or cold limb → immediate emergency vascular care; not all six features must be present. Rest/night pain, non-healing ulcer or gangrene suggests limb-threatening ischaemia and urgent vascular assessment.

Chronic claudication: smoking cessation, supervised exercise, cardiovascular risk treatment, statin and usually clopidogrel for secondary prevention. Persistent symptoms may need revascularisation review. Naftidrofuryl is an option in selected cases after exercise advice and vascular assessment; review benefit and tolerability.
## DVT and swollen legs
Unilateral swelling: DVT, cellulitis, trauma or Baker's cyst; bilateral: HF, renal/liver disease, venous insufficiency, medicines or lymphatic/pelvic causes. Ask about surgery, immobility, cancer, previous VTE, pregnancy, oestrogens and anticoagulant interruption.

**PE symptoms** (dyspnoea, pleuritic pain, haemoptysis, collapse) change urgency immediately. Pregnancy needs the obstetric VTE pathway; do not apply ordinary Wells/D-dimer rules.

For non-pregnant adults use two-level DVT Wells:
- ≥2: proximal ultrasound result within four hours if possible; if delayed, D-dimer before interim therapeutic anticoagulation and scan within 24 hours.
- ≤1: D-dimer first; if delayed >4 hours, consider interim treatment under the pathway. Positive result → ultrasound; negative → alternative diagnosis/safety-net.
- Negative proximal scan plus positive D-dimer in the likely pathway → repeat scan in 6–8 days.

Check contraindications, baseline bloods and treatment suitability; do not delay necessary interim treatment waiting for blood results. Confirmed proximal DVT generally requires at least three months' anticoagulation, then review provocation, recurrence and bleeding risk. Unprovoked VTE warrants history/examination and appropriate tests; not automatic extensive occult-cancer imaging. [NICE NG158](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng158/chapter/Recommendations)
## Varicose veins
Explore pain, swelling, itching, skin change, ulceration, bleeding and cosmetic concerns. Bleeding needs immediate assessment/referral. Symptomatic veins, skin change, superficial thrombosis or current/healed venous ulcer justify vascular review and duplex assessment.

Discuss activity, weight where relevant and leg elevation. With truncal reflux, intervention typically proceeds endothermal ablation → foam if unsuitable → surgery if foam unsuitable. Compression is not routine definitive treatment when intervention is suitable; assess arterial circulation before appropriate compression. [NICE CG168](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg168/chapter/recommendations)
## Abdominal aortic aneurysm
Explain a widened section of the main abdominal artery. Establish screening result, size, symptoms, smoking, family history and the patient's fear of rupture.

New abdominal/back pain, collapse or shock with possible AAA → emergency transfer. Small asymptomatic aneurysms need vascular surveillance and risk reduction, not discharge without follow-up. Consider repair for symptoms, diameter ≥5.5 cm, or >4 cm with growth >1 cm/year; suitability and preference matter. [NICE NG156](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng156/chapter/Recommendations)

## Medicines counselling and monitoring
- **Statins:** distinguish primary prevention (usually atorvastatin 20 mg) from secondary prevention (usually 80 mg, with dose adapted for CKD, interactions/tolerability). Discuss benefit, muscle symptoms, GI effects, interactions and pregnancy plans. Check baseline tests; repeat lipids/transaminases at 2–3 months after initiation/change and transaminases at 12 months. Primary target is >40% non-HDL reduction; secondary target LDL ≤2.0 or non-HDL ≤2.6 mmol/L. Review adherence before escalation. [NICE NG238](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng238/chapter/recommendations)
- **ACE inhibitors/ARBs:** baseline renal function/potassium, recheck after initiation and dose increases; counsel on cough with ACE inhibitors, dizziness and urgent help for angioedema. Pregnancy requires alternative therapy; lactation suitability is drug specific.
- **Beta-blockers:** fatigue, bradycardia, dizziness, cold extremities, sexual/sleep effects; assess airway disease and conduction problems. Do not advise abrupt withdrawal.
- **CCBs:** distinguish rate-limiting drugs from amlodipine-type drugs. Headache/flushing/ankle oedema are common with dihydropyridines. Drug-related oedema does not automatically mean HF and is not routinely treated by adding a diuretic.
- **Digoxin:** nausea, visual disturbance, confusion or arrhythmia may signal toxicity; review renal function, electrolytes and interactions. Levels are indicated for suspected toxicity/specific questions, not routine screening; sample ≥6 hours after a dose.
## Example consultation phrases
### Symptoms, background and risk
- **Chest-pain safety:** “Do you have any pain right now?” / “Have you had this pain at rest?” / “When exactly was the last episode—was it within the last 12 hours?” Ask neutrally and act on the answer.
- **Link lifestyle to the problem:** “Our diet can affect heart health. Could I ask a bit about your diet?”
- **Diet detail:** “Can you take me through what you normally eat in a typical day? How much salt or processed food do you have?”
- **Activity:** “How physically active are you?” Follow up with duration, intensity and symptoms rather than assuming an exercise target suits an acutely unwell person.
- **Palpitations:** “People can mean different things by palpitations. Could you explain what you mean?” / “Could you tap out the rhythm for me?”
- **Clot risk:** “Can I check a few risk factors we think about when someone may have a blood clot?”
- **Support when arranging care:** “You mentioned your husband is at work. Would you like me to ring him or let him know?”
### Plain-language explanations
- **Atherosclerosis:** “Fatty deposits, sometimes called plaques, can build up in the arteries. They narrow the space for blood to flow through.”
- **Angina versus MI:** “Angina is when the heart muscle temporarily isn't getting enough oxygen, often because its arteries are narrowed. A heart attack causes damage to the muscle when its blood supply is severely reduced or blocked.”
- **Why treat angina:** “Angina is a warning sign. Managing it helps control symptoms and reduce the risk of other heart problems.”
- **GTN:** “The spray relaxes and widens blood vessels and can ease the pain within a few minutes. Sit down when you use it, as it can make you dizzy.” Follow with the actual dose and emergency plan.
- **AF:** “It's a heart condition that makes the heartbeat irregular. Sometimes it can be fast as well. We need to manage the symptoms and reduce the risk of complications, particularly stroke.”
- **Rhythm control:** “One option the specialist may consider is trying to get the irregular heartbeat back into a normal rhythm.”
- **Holter monitor:** “It records your heart rhythm over time, so we can link your symptoms with what your heart is doing then.”
- **Echo:** “It's an ultrasound scan of the heart. It looks at the heart's structure and how it is working, including how blood flows through it.”
- **NT-proBNP:** “This is a blood test that helps us work out whether your symptoms could be related to the heart.”
- **Ambulatory BP monitor:** “A cuff on your arm measures your blood pressure automatically at intervals during the day. It helps us see what the pressure is like outside the clinic.”
- **DVT:** “This is a blood clot in one of the deep veins of your leg.”
- **Tamponade:** “Fluid has built up around the heart and is putting pressure on it, so it cannot fill and pump as well. That's why urgent treatment to relieve the pressure is needed.”
- **Varicose veins:** “These are enlarged, twisted veins in the leg.”
### Common questions
- **“Will these tablets be for life?”** “They're usually long-term treatment to control symptoms and reduce risk. We'll monitor how you're doing and review or adjust them if things change.” Do not imply secondary prevention can routinely be stopped.
- **“Can I exercise with angina?”** “Regular gentle activity can help when the condition is stable. Build up appropriately, stop if you get chest pain and follow your action plan.”
- **“Will my heart stop?”** “Most palpitations aren't life-threatening, but we need to check for serious causes. If we find a high-risk problem, we'll arrange prompt treatment.” Reassure only after assessing the presentation.
- **“Can stress or caffeine cause this?”** “They can trigger palpitations in some people. Reducing caffeine and addressing stress may help, alongside checking the cause.”
- **“Will I need a procedure?”** “That depends on what is causing the palpitations. Lifestyle changes or medicine help many people; some problems can be treated with a procedure.”
- **“Is high BP just stress?”** “Stress can affect blood pressure, but there are other reasons it can remain high. We need to assess the overall pattern.”
- **“Is my home machine accurate?”** “What kind of monitor do you have? Where did you get it, and has it been checked?” Confirm cuff size and technique as well.
