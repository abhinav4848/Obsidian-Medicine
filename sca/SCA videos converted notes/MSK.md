## MSK consultation framework
Clarify site, onset/trauma, stiffness/swelling, neurological symptoms, locking/giving way and systemic illness. Assess function rather than pain score alone: walking, stairs, dressing, sleep, dominant hand, work/driving and hobbies. Ask relevant cancer/infection history, steroid use and previous treatment. Examine the joint, neurovascular status and relevant adjacent joints; remote observation cannot exclude fracture/infection.
## Neck pain and whiplash
Significant trauma, midline tenderness, neurological deficit, myelopathy, infection or malignancy features need prompt appropriate assessment. Use validated cervical-spine injury criteria when relevant; do not manipulate a potentially unstable injury.

Uncomplicated mechanical pain/whiplash: explanation, appropriate analgesia, graded normal activity and work adjustment. Avoid prolonged immobilisation. Persistent or progressive symptoms require reassessment; MRI is not automatic solely because several weeks have passed.
## Low back pain and sciatica
Establish exact site/radiation, onset, progression, activity/rest pattern, stiffness, neurological symptoms and abdominal/urinary/reproductive clues. Consider inflammatory back pain, fracture, infection, cancer, AAA/renal or pancreatic causes.

**Cauda equina concern:** new bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction, altered saddle sensation or significant bilateral/progressive neurological symptoms → emergency assessment under the spinal pathway. Do not wait for complete retention or every classic sign. Suspected metastatic cord compression is also an emergency, particularly with cancer history and new weakness/gait/sphincter change.

For uncomplicated low back pain: explain the working diagnosis, encourage activity and return to usual function, assess psychosocial barriers/yellow flags and consider stratified exercise/physiotherapy. Do not routinely image in primary care. Oral NSAID may help after GI/cardiorenal risk assessment, at lowest effective dose/shortest duration; weak opioid is a selected acute alternative if NSAID unsuitable, not routine chronic treatment.

Sciatica has radiating nerve-root symptoms; explain that neurological reassessment matters if weakness progresses. Do not offer gabapentinoids, oral steroids or benzodiazepines for sciatica, or opioids for chronic sciatica. Existing dependence-forming treatment needs a supported review, not abrupt withdrawal. Selected severe acute/refractory cases may need specialist intervention. [NICE NG59](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59/chapter/recommendations)
## Shoulder

| Pattern | Working approach |
|---|---|
| Global restriction of active **and passive** movement, especially external rotation | Frozen shoulder; diabetes/thyroid context, analgesia, staged exercises/physio ± injection; recovery can be prolonged |
| Painful arc, pain on resisted movement, relatively preserved passive range | Rotator-cuff-related pain; load modification and rehabilitation; acute traumatic marked weakness needs early assessment |
| Local AC joint tenderness, pain across body | AC pathology; conservative care, reassess persistent/uncertain disease |
| Instability/dislocation | Acute deformity needs ED reduction/neurovascular assessment; recurrent instability needs specialist/rehab plan |
Check referred neck/cardiac pain and infection. Do not promise every shoulder condition will resolve by a fixed date.
## Elbow, wrist and hand
- **Lateral epicondylalgia:** overuse-related lateral pain/grip difficulty; modify load, appropriate analgesia and strengthening/physio. Routine steroid injection has limited longer-term value; avoid presenting it as a guaranteed cure.
- **Carpal tunnel:** median distribution tingling, nocturnal symptoms and grip weakness; assess pregnancy, diabetes, thyroid and inflammatory disease when indicated. Neutral night splint, selected steroid injection; persistent deficit/thenar wasting or failed conservative care needs specialist assessment.
- **Scaphoid injury:** snuffbox/scaphoid tenderness after injury can be significant despite an initially normal X-ray. Immobilisation and appropriate urgent imaging/fracture follow-up are needed; do not reassure solely on the first film.
- **Distal radius fracture:** deformity/tenderness after a fall → acute assessment, imaging and neurovascular care. Include bone health, falls, ADLs and support after immobilisation.
## Hip and knee
Hip OA often causes groin pain and reduced internal rotation; assess gait and adjacent spine/knee. Manage activity, weight where relevant, aids, physiotherapy and appropriate analgesia; joint replacement referral depends on symptoms/function and response. Suspected hip fracture after a fall/inability to bear weight needs emergency assessment even without obvious deformity.

Adolescent knee pain may be Osgood–Schlatter (tibial tubercle) or patellofemoral pain; assess hip causes and red flags first. Relative load adjustment and targeted rehabilitation are central. Do not routinely propose arthroscopy for uncomplicated Osgood–Schlatter.

Acute knee injury: mechanism, pop, speed of swelling, locking and instability guide assessment. A truly locked knee or significant injury needs urgent review. Ottawa knee criteria support X-ray decisions: age ≥55, isolated patellar/fibular-head tenderness, inability to flex 90° or inability to bear weight for four steps at injury and assessment, applied in the validated setting.
## Ankle, foot and Achilles
Use Ottawa rules appropriately: malleolar-zone pain plus relevant posterior-edge/tip tenderness or inability to bear weight supports ankle imaging; midfoot pain plus navicular/base-fifth tenderness or inability to bear weight supports foot imaging. Rules do not replace judgement for severe injury, children outside validation or neurovascular concerns.

Sprain/strain: short protection, symptom control, compression/elevation where suitable, then progressive loading/movement and rehabilitation; avoid prolonged unnecessary rest. Consider fracture, syndesmotic injury or tendon rupture if recovery is atypical.

**Achilles rupture:** acute snap/giving way, weak plantarflexion or abnormal calf-squeeze test → urgent injury/orthopaedic pathway. Tendinopathy: gradual load-related pain, managed with load modification and progressive rehabilitation; do not offer tendon steroid injection casually because of rupture risk.

**Plantar heel pain:** often worst first steps after rest. Explain load, stretching, suitable footwear/heel support, activity adjustment and weight support where appropriate. Persistent/atypical cases need reassessment and possible physio/podiatry.
## Bone infection and tumours
Focal bone pain/swelling with fever, systemic illness or an infected wound can signal osteomyelitis; urgent hospital assessment is needed. MRI/cultures and prolonged treatment are specialist decisions, not a fixed antibiotic duration for every case.

Persistent unexplained bone pain/swelling, night pain, mass or pathological fracture requires investigation and cancer consideration. Cord compression, fracture or severe systemic illness changes urgency immediately. Explain the need for imaging/specialist review, interim pain relief and practical support while awaiting assessment.
## Example consultation phrases
- **Pain and coping:** “When people have pain for a long time, they sometimes turn to things they think will help, such as alcohol, smoking or drugs. Is any of that relevant for you?” (V38, 14:58)
- **Explain the examination:** “I'd like to look at how you walk and stand, check the movements in your back, and examine the nerves in your legs.” Select the examination appropriate to the presentation. (V38, 16:24)
- **Straight-leg raise:** “Raising the leg stretches the sciatic nerve. Reproducing that particular pain can give us a clue about whether the nerve is irritated.” (V38, 22:44)
- **Carpal tunnel:** “A nerve runs through a narrow space at the wrist. If it gets compressed there, it can cause the tingling and other symptoms you've described.” (V39, 03:58)
- **Sprain versus strain:** “A sprain affects a ligament, which connects bones at a joint. A strain affects a muscle or tendon.” (V39, 23:05)
- **Plantar fascia:** “Plantar means the sole of the foot, and the fascia is the strong band of tissue along it. Irritation of that tissue can cause this heel pain.” (V39, 27:45)
- **Possible bone infection:** “I'm concerned about an infection in the bone. We need hospital assessment today; they may do blood tests, cultures and a scan.” (V39, 29:19–30:08)
