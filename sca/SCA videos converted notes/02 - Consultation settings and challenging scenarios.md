## Telephone and video
### Opening logistics
- **Identity:** confirm **2 points of identity (DoB & address)**. Having a name on the record does not establish who answered the telephone.
- **Callback number:** confirm how to reconnect if the call drops.
- **Geography:** “Can I just check whereabouts are you calling from right now?”
- **Clarify the location:** if the answer is “at my mom's house”, ask: “Can I ask where that is?” Establish the actual location, distance from the practice and whether they are abroad; obtain an address if urgent help may be needed.
- **Appropriateness of time:** “Can I just check if you've a few minutes right now? Is this a good time to chat?”
- **Environment and privacy:** establish whether they are at home, at work, in a café or somewhere else; whether anyone can hear the conversation or see the screen; and whether they are comfortable to continue.
- **Who is present:** identify anyone accompanying them and agree their involvement.
### Telephone — build the clinical picture
Use the usual red flags, psychosocial history and ICE. Ask about relevant findings you would otherwise see, and explain why you need the questions.
> “Mr. X, you've mentioned a cough, I'm just going ask you quite a few questions about that cough just to see if everything is okay.”
- Can they count their pulse rate?
- Do they have a smartwatch that gives their current heart rate?
- Can you hear breathlessness or coughing? Say what you have noticed.
- “Gosh. You sound like you're a little short of breath.”
- “I can hear you coughing in the background.”

Verbalise empathy and reflect changes in tone; body language cannot carry the response over the telephone.
- “Mr. X, you do sound a little bit worried.”
- “Mr. X, I can see this is really difficult for you.”

See [[01c- groundwork- relating to others#Reasoning, relevance and reflection]]
### Video — use the additional information
- Confirm identity, including name, age and date of birth as appropriate, plus geography, a suitable time and a private environment even though you can see them.
- Look towards the camera regularly to maintain eye contact; avoid continually looking down at the screen or notes.
- Can they count their pulse or use a smartwatch?
- Can they show an accessible rash?
- Can they show the swollen wrist and compare it with the other side?
- Could showing a result help them follow the explanation? Use only facilities available in the consultation; do not assume screen-sharing is available in the assessment.
Remote observations and patient measurements may help; they do not replace an examination that is needed.
### Assessment and management
Give the working diagnosis/differentials, offer an explanation and put the issues on the table. Decide whether you need to take the lead. A plan to arrange examination still needs reasoning, logistics, interim care and appropriate follow-up.
- Is there something requiring action **now**, **today** or on an **urgent** basis?
- Does the patient need to be seen? When, where and by whom?
- Why is examination needed, and what will you assess?
- What investigations or treatment might follow that assessment?
- What is likely to happen at the practice, clinic or hospital?
- What follow-up is needed?
- What deterioration before assessment would require earlier help?
Put necessary follow-up and safety-netting on the issue list early, then deliver them.
### Transport, support and access — questions to cover
- Where are they now in relation to the practice or hospital?
- Who are they with **right now**?
- Who do they usually live with?
- Do they drive?
- Are they safe to drive in this situation?
- If they should not drive, can someone else drive them?
- Who else is around and able to help?
- Who will bring them to the practice or take them to hospital?
- Do you need to arrange an ambulance? What level of urgency is required?
- If they remain at home unwell, who will be around **overnight**?
- Are there relatives nearby?
- Can they contact those relatives quickly in the middle of the night?
- Is it safe for them to be alone over the next few days? Is additional support needed?
- Can they collect the prescription or medication?
- How will they obtain the medication you want them to take?
Before finishing, check that the plan actually progresses their care: assessment, treatment, access, responsibilities and review should be clear.
## Angry patient
### Let them finish, then give an early positive response
Allow the initial account without jumping in with explanations or apologies. Listen for the point at which they invite your response. Then acknowledge the problem and offer something constructive.
- “Look, I'm really here to help. I'm sure there's something we can sort out by the end.”
- “I can see there's been a very difficult time. I'm here to see how we can improve things.”
Be specific about what you can look into; avoid promising an outcome you cannot establish.
### Acknowledge again when frustration rises
- “I can really see this is frustrating. Just a few questions to see how we can help you.”
- “Once again, I can see this is so frustrating. I know these questions you've answered before. Just a couple more to see how I can help you best.”
**Swapping chairs:**
> “You know what, Mr. X, if I've been waiting for six weeks for an appointment like this and I've been promised a few weeks, I'd be just as annoyed as you.”
**Acknowledge the importance:**
> “You know what, you're right, Mr. X. This is an important issue and I'm really glad that you brought it to our attention. Absolutely, this is something that does need looking into and I'm glad you've come to me about this.”
### Still obtain the necessary history
Signpost the questions, especially if they have answered them before.
> “Mr. X, I know you've had a lot of these questions asked before, but I'm just gonna go through very quickly a couple of yes, no questions that can help me to understand what's going on.”
For a GI presentation, the example questions include:
- “Have you lost weight?”
- “Have you had any vomiting?”
- “Have you vomited blood?”
These are examples within the relevant red-flag history, rather than a complete GI checklist. Still establish the psychosocial impact and ICE.
### Apology, resolution and learning
Apologise for distress or an established error; establish the facts before attributing blame.
- “I'm really sorry that you're in this situation.”
- “I'm really sorry that you had to go through all of this.”
- “It's really hard for me to comment on what you've said without me having been there. I can see this must have been really difficult. I'll certainly try and find out exactly what happened so we can take it forward.”
Move towards an agreed resolution without dismissing what happened:
- “I know this has been really tough for you but let's see how we can resolve it.”
- “Gosh, I know it's been really difficult but let's try and find a way to figure this out.”
Explain what an internal review involves rather than only naming an incident-reporting process:
> “You know when these kind of things happen, we don't just ignore them. We try and figure out what's happened. So we're gonna get together as a team. I'll talk to my colleagues, figure out what happened, and we'll see how we can improve things.”
Agree actions, responsibility and a response time. Be open about the complaints process and support their right to complain. Use boundaries and obtain help if there are threats or abuse.
## Embarrassed or hesitant patient
### Create an environment of safety
- Allow time and silence; keep eye contact, nod and remain engaged.
- Thank them for making contact.
- Acknowledge the difficulty and use neutral language.
- Normalise common problems without dismissing their experience.
- Explore what would help them speak; do not assume that silence means a confidentiality concern.
- “Take your time, there's no rush at all, I'm here whenever you're ready.”
- “Mr. X, look, I know this is really difficult. I just wanna say thank you for calling us today. It's not easy to talk to a stranger about these things. I just wanna thank you for doing that because I wanna see how we can help.”
- “I can see this is clearly a challenging topic to talk about. I do appreciate the difficulty you must have had in discussing this or talking to me about this.”
- “If this was me talking to a stranger about something that was embarrassing, look, I'd find it really hard to do it as well. So I do see it from your point of view. Take your time. I am here to try and help.”
- “What you're talking to me about is actually really common. We tend to see this thing much more than you might imagine on a day to day basis.”
### Offer relevant alternatives
- “Would you prefer if I booked you with a female doctor?”
- “Would you prefer to come in and talk face to face?”
Offer choices that fit the person's difficulty and preferences; consider a chaperone when appropriate.
### Confidentiality
> “Mr. X, of course, the things that we talk about today will remain confidential unless of course there is risk involved.”
Explain confidentiality and its relevant limits without promising absolute secrecy. An early stock confidentiality line may miss the actual reason for hesitancy; first give them time and explore the difficulty. [GMC confidentiality](https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/confidentiality).
## Disengaged patient
Look for disengagement early: limited attention, repeatedly asking you to repeat yourself, looking elsewhere or dismissing the problem. Acknowledge that the call may not be what they wanted to do.
- “I can really see that you don't wanna be talking about your kidney results right now.”
- “I can appreciate there are many other things that you'd probably be doing than talking about your kidney results.”
### Find what matters to them
Still assess safety and explain the relevance of psychosocial questions. Explore beliefs, fears, expectations, work, children, family, exams or travel; one of these may make the health problem meaningful to them.
- **Ideas:** “Now, Mr. X, look, I know we discussed your blood pressure many, many times in the past. So rather than me telling you lots about it, do you mind telling me what you know about raised blood pressure? Just so I'm not gonna bore you overgoing things that you already know.”
- **Concerns:** “Mr. X, look, I know there's not a lot of things that you seem to be worried about when it comes to your blood pressure, but I'm just curious. Is there anything that does worry you about your blood pressure?”
- **Expectations:** “Look. I really know that you don't wanna be talking about this right now. You don't wanna be on this call. I get it. But now that you're here, now that we are on this call, is there anything that you think we can do to help?”
Signpost repeated safety questions:
> “Look, I know this is really frustrating. I know you've had these questions asked thousands of times before, but I just need to get them done to make sure nothing has changed. I know it's frustrating to go through these again and again, but give me a bit of time. Let me try and get this done as quick as we can.”
### Bring their concern back in management
Frame the issue list and options around the thing they value. If their concern is kidney disease, link the blood-pressure discussion to that concern:
> “Look, I know that you had mentioned about kidney problems and dialysis. Let's see what we can do to try and prevent this. I've got a few ideas in my mind. Look, let's try and look at things like weight, exercise, medication.”
**Link the plan to work when that matters to them:**
> “You mentioned you've got a really hectic job that you love. Look, let's try and reduce the chances of that being impacted in the future by kidney problems. Let's try and sort that out today so your job is not gonna cause you some problems later on.”
Use an achievable next step and respect informed choices. Do not rely on repeating the clinical importance until they agree.
## Demands for tests, referrals or treatment
### Acknowledge the request and establish the data
Acknowledge the actual request regularly so they know it has been heard. Avoid agreeing or refusing before you have assessed the situation; do not promise an inappropriate investigation to gain cooperation.
- “I can understand that you want an MRI scan and it's certainly something that we can focus on.”
- “When people ask for MRI scans, lots goes through their mind. Can I just find out what's going through your mind?”
- “Mr. X, when people request MRI scans, like we said, there are certain clinical questions that we tend to ask.”
- “When people ask for MRI scans, something is usually worrying them. Is that the case with you?”
### Questions and assessment to cover
- What do they know about the requested test or referral?
- What do they expect it to show or achieve?
- What is worrying them?
- Apart from that request, did they want anything else, such as pain relief or a referral?
- What red flags are present or absent?
- Is an examination needed before deciding? For headache, consider the relevant observations and neurological examination.
- What previous assessment, treatment or investigation has already occurred?
Explain why the questions are necessary to make the decision or complete an appropriate referral:
> “Mr. X, I know this is really frustrating, but for me to sign any kind of MRI scan request, there are certain questions that we need to ask as doctors because if we don't put that detail on the form, it might just get rejected anyway.”
Discuss other tests only when they could genuinely answer the clinical question better:
> “But sometimes there are other tests, maybe better tests that can actually do the job better in your situation. But for me to figure out if that's the case, I need to ask you a few questions first.”
### Explain the decision and keep helping
Discuss usefulness, limitations and relevant risks; the answer should follow from the assessment.
> “Look, Mr. X, I'm really sorry. I know you want an MRI scan but after discussing things with you, I really don't think that that's the most appropriate step at this point because x y z but let's see what else we can do to help the situation.”
Replace **x y z** with the actual reasoning. Offer a positive next step rather than ending at refusal.
For a tonsillectomy request:
> “Look I can see this is really important to you. I can see that you really want a referral to get your tonsils taken out. I don't think that's something that would be done but look, let me speak to one of the ENT registrars or ENT consultants just to see what they think.”
For an MRI request:
> “Mr. X, I really don't think that an MRI scan is necessary here but I can see that you're really keen. Let me maybe speak to one of the neurology doctors or one of the radiology doctors and get a bit of advice from them.”
Seek advice from someone who can add value to the actual problem; explain the appropriate second-opinion route.

## Refusing recommended care
### Establish the recommendation and the reason for refusal
- What clinical findings support the recommendation?
- What does the patient understand about the condition and the proposed treatment, investigation, referral or admission?
- Are they frightened of a test, hospital, medication or side effect?
- Have they had an adverse experience, or heard about one in the family?
- Do they feel well and therefore doubt the diagnosis or need for treatment?
- Who is at home and what support do they have?
- Who have they discussed this with?
- Do they care for a parent, child, another dependent or a pet?
- Are work, an important meeting, a deadline or financial pressures preventing attendance?
- Are there travel, holiday, wedding or other commitments?
Do not assume the reason. Explore a disclosed barrier properly, because it may be something you can help resolve.
### Explain the advice and the serious consequence clearly
State what you recommend and why. Explain the material risks of declining, using the actual concern rather than a vague warning that it could be serious.
**Admission:**
> “Mr. X, the reason that we advise admission in this type of situation is that because if it is a stroke, for example, or a heart attack, potentially this could end up being a life threatening situation.”
**Treatment for suspected temporal arteritis:**
> “The reason that we advise this medication is that if we don't take it, potentially, you could be at risk of permanent vision loss.”
**Investigation for a possible bleeding ulcer:**
> “Mr. X, the reason that we're advising you to have this test is that if we don't rule out, let's say, a bleeding ulcer, ultimately, it could end up in a potentially life threatening situation.”
Use the risk that applies to this patient. Check understanding and allow questions.
### Respect the decision and find the safest acceptable plan
If capacity is in doubt, assess it for this decision at this time, support communication and apply the relevant national framework. A decision you consider unwise does not itself establish incapacity. Respect an informed, voluntary refusal by an adult with capacity; repeated warnings are not a way to force agreement. [GMC decision making and consent](https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/decision-making-and-consent).
- Would discussing it with someone they trust help?
- “You mentioned your wife had some thoughts about this. Is it worth having a chat with them at this point before you make your final decision?”
- Who else could look after the parent they care for?
- Who else could collect the children?
- Could someone help with the pet, work commitment or transport?
- Is there a clinically acceptable alternative test, treatment, assessment or review arrangement?
Address barriers and offer the safest acceptable alternative. Agree specific access, safety-netting and follow-up, document the discussion and obtain urgent advice when capacity or immediate safety is unresolved.
## Bad news and abnormal results
### Establish the starting point
- What has changed since the previous assessment? Recheck relevant red flags without automatically retelling the whole history.
- What does the patient already think is happening?
- Have they considered cancer or another serious diagnosis?
- What is worrying them?
- What were they hoping the result or consultation would provide?
- Who is at home or regularly around them?
- Have they already discussed the problem with anyone?
- Would they like someone else involved in the discussion?
- Would they feel more comfortable discussing it face to face, with support?
- What relevant risk factors might they ask about after receiving the result, such as smoking?
- What work, holiday, family or other commitments might affect the next steps?
Use what they already know to pitch the explanation. Introduce the concern sensitively and clearly, allowing time for their response and management afterwards.
### Suspected cancer — abnormal chest X-ray
> “Mr. X, we've got an X-ray result in front of me, and I'll be honest, it's not a 100% normal. We've seen what we call a shadow in the top right of your lung. Now when we see shadows on lung X rays, it could mean lots of different things. Best case scenario, it could be something like an infection. We don't tend to worry about those things too much. But, Mr. X, when we see shadows like this on an X-ray, we just need to make sure we're not missing what we call the worst case scenario here and by that I mean things like cancer.”
### Suspected melanoma — changing mole
> “Mr. X, you've got something called a mole. I'm sure you've heard of a mole before, but it looks like that mole has changed recently. Now when we see a mole that changes like this, there are many different reasons. Best case scenario, it could just be a mole that's changed. Sometimes they do and we don't worry about them always. But, Mr. X, when you see a mole that's changed like this, we just need to rule out what we call the worst case scenario here and by that I mean things like cancer.”
### Confirmed cancer — state the result
> “Unfortunately, in this situation, having a look at the result, it has proved to be a cancer.”
Distinguish a suspected diagnosis from a confirmed result. Use the actual report; do not invent stage, prognosis or curability, or suggest uncertainty about a result that is already confirmed.
### Allow the reaction
Pause and respond to the patient's emotion: upset, fear, anger, silence or another reaction. Avoid carrying on with the next explanation while they are still processing the news.
> “I can see this is a lot to take in, I can see you weren't expecting that information. Take your time. There's no time to rush.”
### Put the whole situation on the table
> “Mr. X, look, I know there's a lot of things going on right now. There are a few important things I think we need to have a bit of a chat about. Firstly, I wanna talk about what happens next. Clinics, appointments, tests, referrals. I'll go through all of that in detail. Secondly, I know you had a few things on your mind. You're worried about your holiday coming up next week. We can talk about that. There's things that we can help with that as well. And thirdly, obviously, this is big news. Now you mentioned your wife earlier and you've discussed this with her. Look, if you need any support around that, telling her, discussing things with her, we can talk about that as well.”
Bring back the patient's actual concerns and support needs rather than delivering only a referral explanation.
### Questions you may be asked
- “Doctor, am I gonna die?”
- “Doctor, how long have I got left?”
- “Doctor, is this curable?”
**Acknowledge → be honest → take the next step:**
- “Look, Mr. X, I know I've thrown a lot of information at you. I can understand that information like this can cause a lot of questions in your mind. That's quite normal.”
- “Unfortunately, I'll be honest at this stage, I don't have the answer to that question right now.”
- “But look, let's talk about the things that we can do to get closer to those answers for you.”
### Keep communication open afterwards
Specify how they can contact the team and who can help between now and the next appointment.
- “Don't hesitate to ask us if you have any questions going forward. I'm sure many more questions are gonna come to your mind, just contact us.”
- “You mentioned that your wife is with you tonight, if she has any questions she can get in touch.”
Agree the patient's permission for involving family. Check who will support them later that day or overnight.
## Multiple problems
**OEP: organise → empathise → prioritise.**
### Organise
Get the complete agenda early so that additional issues do not keep appearing after you have started detailed management.
> “Mr. X, it seems like a lot of things are going on. Let's clarify exactly the things that you were hoping to get through today. Let's get out that list. Let's go through it.”
### Empathise
Acknowledge the burden before moving directly to prioritisation.
> “Well done for managing all of this. Seems like a lot's been going on.”
### Prioritise
**Clinician-led when safety requires it:**
> “Mr. X, I know you mentioned quite a few things there and I'll try to address them, but one thing shouts out to me. You mentioned you had a bit of pressure in your chest a few days ago. I'd like to talk about that first.”
**Patient-led when there is no overriding clinical priority:**
> “Now Mr. X, there's a lot of things there. Were there any of those that you wanted to focus on first?”
### Explain a staged plan in terms of their care
> “Look, Mr. X, I can see there's lot of things to go through. I think if we try and cover all of this today, we probably wouldn't be giving any of them the importance that they need or they deserve in fact. Shall we try and focus on a few of them properly today, get them done, get them ticked off so that at least they don't cause you any trouble and then maybe we can get you back and do the rest at another point.”
Avoid making the explanation about your workload or how difficult the consultation is for you. Assess the chosen issues and make a specific plan for the remainder, after checking they can safely wait.
## Very talkative patient
Allow an initial account without showing annoyance. Then acknowledge what they have said, use their own words to return to an important point and ask focused questions.
**Acknowledge and focus:**
> “I can see there's lots going on, lots going through your mind. You've got a lot that you wanna share with me. Look, you mentioned symptoms just there. Can I ask you a few specific ones?”
**Slow the pace:**
> “Let's try and break this down a little bit. Can I ask you a few specific questions about your pain?”
**Return to an earlier important symptom:**
> “Now you mentioned your knee pain just there. Can I go back to that again a bit more detail?”
Do not mirror their speed. Obtain the necessary assessment, give the working conclusion, outline the issues and act on the priority. Make a safe plan for anything deferred.
## Behaviour change
### Questions to cover
- What do they know about the relevant behaviour and its health effects?
- What worries them about it?
- Was there anything particular they hoped you could do to help?
- What relevant red flags or symptoms need assessment, even if the booking says smoking cessation or lifestyle advice?
- In a smoking-cessation consultation, ask about chest symptoms, weight loss and breathing symptoms.
- How long have they been smoking?
- What do they smoke?
- What are their reasons for smoking?
- Have they tried stopping before? What happened?
- Why are they considering change **now**?
- For alcohol: what is the pattern and amount, and are there signs of dependence or withdrawal risk?
### Broader lifestyle and practical context
- **Smoking.**
- **Alcohol.**
- **Diet.**
- **Exercise.**
- **Weight.**
- **Stress.**
Also establish:
- Who is at home and able to support a change?
- What influence do household members have on the behaviour?
- Would someone else be willing to make changes with them?
- Do work hours, night shifts, finances or other practical barriers affect their choices?
Put the main behaviour, associated symptoms and other relevant concerns on the issue list before going into the detailed plan.
> “So there are a few important things to talk about today. Number one, of course, we'll talk about smoking and we'll talk about the kind of things that we can do to try and help you come off smoking but also you mentioned you'd have a couple of symptoms and it might be worth just focusing on those a little bit as well even though you're really keen to stop smoking.”
### Make it a discussion
Explore whether the person responds more to the benefits of change or to understanding the consequences of continuing. Discuss relevant risks accurately and without threats; connect them to the person's goals.
**Benefits of change:**
> “Mr. X, there are actually lots of benefits that you can find through changing your diet or altering your weight, things like your cholesterol going down, your weight will go down, your risk of hypertension will go down, your diabetes risk will go down, your energy levels will increase.”
**Consequences of continuing:**
> “Mr. X, if we don't manage to change your diet or your alcohol or your smoking for example, unfortunately things might happen. Blood pressure might go up, your cholesterol level might go higher, your diabetes risk increases, your energy level might decrease.”
Tailor the benefits and risks to the actual behaviour and clinical context; they are not guaranteed individual outcomes.
### Practical solutions and small changes
**Activity around work:**
> “Look, I recognize it's really difficult to exercise regularly with all the modern day challenges. How about maybe walking upstairs at work for example, you mentioned you work on the 4th Floor as opposed to going on the lift.”
**Food at work:**
> “You mentioned that canteen serves unhealthy food. I understand that a lot of canteens sadly do at the moment, but how about considering packed lunches or maybe even ordering food via an app instead at least then you get more control about what you're eating.”
**Gradual change:**
> “Habits are very difficult to change, we do realize that but sometimes making small and regular changes are often easier than one big change that doesn't last. So let's talk about a graded program about how we can change things slowly.”
### Involve support that fits their circumstances
- “You mentioned you live with a wife. Does she go to the gym? Could you maybe go at the same time?”
- Could the children help with cooking and choosing healthy food?
- Would a dietitian, exercise support, a smoking-cessation service or stress-management support help?
- What is practical and accessible for this person?
Agree a small, specific next step and review. Assess dependence and withdrawal risk before advising someone dependent on alcohol to stop suddenly.
## Medicines not taken as prescribed
Consider actual medicine use in chronic disease, polypharmacy, poor control, adverse effects or cues such as preferring natural treatments or disliking pill taking. Establish what they take without accusation.
### Safety and practical questions
- Is the underlying condition worsening?
- Have they noticed memory loss or confusion?
- Can they reach and retrieve the medicines? Are they upstairs when the person struggles with stairs?
- Does shoulder pain prevent reaching them?
- Do painful or rheumatoid hands make opening packaging or lids difficult?
- Would a suitable day-by-day system help?
- Could the pharmacist help with packaging or administration?
- Do they rely on a relative or carer to give the medicines?
- What happens when that person cannot attend?
### Beliefs and concerns
- Are they **having** adverse effects, or worried about developing them?
- Has a relative had a problem with the same medicine?
- Are they worried about dependence or addiction?
- Do they think the medicine is not helping because they feel no different?
- Do they plan to wait until symptoms appear before taking treatment?
- Do they doubt the diagnosis because they feel well?
### Agree a workable alternative
Explain what the medicine achieves, its relevant benefits and risks, and the serious consequences of declining where applicable. Respect an informed refusal by an adult with capacity; see [[#Refusing recommended care]].
- Are there acceptable alternatives?
- Would a dose change be appropriate?
- Would a modified-release preparation help, such as metformin when adverse effects are the barrier?
- Would another medication or formulation be preferable?
- Would a time-limited trial with an agreed review help assess benefit or adverse effects?
**Agree a trial and review:**
> “Let's try maybe this tablet for let's say a few weeks and then see you back and see if your symptoms remain the same or if your side effects have cleared.”
Tailor practical support and pharmacist involvement; dosette boxes suit selected needs rather than every patient. Review the outcome of the agreed change.

## Emergency and admission
### Emergency — act immediately
Recognise the need for emergency help early and take the lead. Activate help while gathering the information needed to manage the immediate situation; do not complete a routine history first.
**Location and support:**
- Where is the patient?
- Are they at home or at work?
- Are they alone?
- Who is around them right now?
- Can someone stay with them while help arrives?
- Can they call an ambulance, or do you need to arrange it?
- Does anyone else need to be informed, such as a family member or friend?
**Immediate management:**
- What emergency action is required now?
- Does someone with anaphylaxis have an adrenaline auto-injector available, and is someone able to help them use it?
- If someone is having a seizure, can the person present give appropriate first aid and protect them from injury?
Make the emergency ambulance requirement explicit when indicated. Give condition-specific immediate advice and confirm the practical plan.
### Admission — establish urgency and barriers
Assess relevant red flags, psychosocial circumstances and ICE. Keep checking whether the situation actually requires emergency help.
- For a history of chest pain: **“Is there any pain right now?”**
- Who is with them now, and who is usually available?
- Are there blockers such as a meeting, holiday, exam, caring duty or work deadline?
- When is the commitment, what does it involve and who else knows or can help?
- What did they think was happening when they contacted you?
- What are they worried about?
- Had they considered that hospital assessment might be needed?
### Say hospital today early
> “There are a few important things that are going on that you've told me x y z but I think first and foremost because of what you've been saying, I do think we need to get this checked in hospital today.”
Allow time to respond to the recommendation. If they resist, explain the serious risk, explore the barrier and follow [[#Refusing recommended care]].
### Practical admission questions
- Who can take them to hospital?
- Is someone with them who can help?
- Do you need to contact a partner or relative?
- “You mentioned your husband's at work. Do you need me to give them a call?”
- Is it safe for them to drive?
- “I don't think it's probably the best idea for you to drive. You mentioned your husband. Could he take you?”
- Is an ambulance the appropriate transport, and what urgency is required?
- Could they contact their employer, or would help contacting the employer be useful?
- Could someone else collect the children or cover other caring duties?
- “Can I give you a note or a letter?”
Choose transport according to the clinical urgency and local arrangements. Address driving before the patient assumes they can take themselves. Explain likely examination, investigations and treatment at hospital without promising a discharge time.
### Anticipate the patient's questions
- “Doctor, am I gonna have to stay in overnight?”
- “Doctor, can I just go in tomorrow?”
- “Doctor, what about my job?”
- “Doctor, who's gonna look after my cat?”
Use the support and practical information already gathered. When an answer is uncertain, say so and explain the next step.
## Urgent referral
### Establish the concern and the patient's perspective
- What red flags or result justify the referral?
- Has anything changed since the last assessment?
- Who is around the patient and able to support them?
- Are a holiday, wedding, exam, work or caring commitments likely to affect attendance?
- Have they considered cancer, where relevant?
- Is cancer something they are worried about?
- What did they think needed to happen next?
**Signpost the clinical questions:**
> “Mr. X, when it comes to indigestion, there are a couple of yes no questions, medical type questions that I need to run through.”
**Take the lead in management:**
> “I think first and foremost, because of symptom x y z, I think an urgent referral is something that we need to talk about first.”
Explain the reason for referral clearly, including cancer when relevant. Allow time for the reaction and bring the patient's other concerns back into the plan.
### Logistics between now and the clinic
- Who will go with them to the clinic?
- “You mentioned that you don't live with anybody right now. Is there anyone that you can go with?”
- What examinations, investigations or other next steps are likely at the clinic?
- How and when should they expect appointment information?
- Whom should they contact if it does not arrive by the agreed time?
- Who will be around them while they wait, including overnight if they are distressed?
- If they lack support, what support can be arranged?
- How can they ask further questions before the appointment?
- What change in symptoms requires earlier assessment?
**Practical help with an exam commitment:**
> “I'm happy to write a letter to your college or university for example.”
**Chasing an appointment:**
> “If you don't hear anything about an appointment within a couple of days, give us a call. We'll chase it up.”
Agree the actual chasing interval with the referral pathway. Explain what can be done about travel, insurance, work or other barriers.
**Support while waiting:**
> “Call me if you have any questions in between.”
Specify the team's available contact route and out-of-hours arrangements rather than implying continuous personal availability.
### Explain the actual referral timescale
Use the relevant local/national pathway. **Two-week wait is not a universal appointment guarantee.** In England, the Faster Diagnosis Standard concerns being told a diagnosis or having cancer ruled out within **28 days** of an urgent suspected-cancer referral. [NHS England cancer waiting-times guidance](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/national-cancer-waiting-times-monitoring-dataset-guidance/).
## Relative or carer
### Orientate the call
- Who is calling, and what is their relationship to the patient?
- Where is the patient right now?
- Are they in the same house, or in a different city?
- Why is the patient not on the call themselves?
- Are they scared, unaware of the concern or unable to call?
- Do they know the relative is speaking to you?
- Have they discussed making this call?
- Are they happy for it to happen, or did they ask the relative to call?
- Have they discussed permission to talk about medical information and records?
- What has already been discussed with the patient?
- What is the patient thinking about the situation?
### Clinical questions and confidentiality
Take the relevant clinical history from the caller and clarify how they know the information. For example:
- **Memory concern:** head injury, headache and neurological symptoms.
- **Breathlessness or cough:** haemoptysis, travel and fever, alongside the relevant symptom history and red flags.
Listen to concerns even when you cannot disclose confidential information. Establish and document the patient's permission and its scope before sharing records, or identify another justified basis for disclosure. Avoid inadvertently confirming confidential facts. Consent does not universally have to be written. [GMC information sharing and listening to those close to the patient](https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/confidentiality/using-and-disclosing-patient-information-for-direct-care).
If permission has not been established:
> “Look, I'm really sorry but until I have permission to do so I'm not gonna be able to talk about their medical records.”
Check the available consent information and clarify it with the patient when needed. You can still gather information, give general advice, address safety concerns and arrange patient contact.
### Understand the support network
- Does the caller live with the patient?
- Do they care for the patient?
- Does the patient care for them?
- What is the household and support setup?
- Who else is already involved?
- Are other relatives or friends helping?
- Are paid carers or other professionals involved?
### Double ICE — all six questions
- **Caller's ideas:** “What did you think about all of this?”
- **Patient's ideas:** “Do you know what your father was thinking about all of this?”
- **Caller's concerns:** “Is there anything that's particularly worrying you about all of this?”
- **Patient's concerns:** “Do you know if your father had any worries about what was going on?”
- **Caller's expectations:** “Is there anything that you thought we could do in this situation to take it forward?”
- **Patient's expectations:** “Do you know if your father had any ideas about how we might take this further?”
Keep the patient's reported perspective distinct from the caller's assumptions.
### Ask about the caller's wellbeing too
- “How are you coping with all of this?”
- “How is it affecting you?”
- “Who are you talking to?”
- “What's your support network?”
Acknowledge the effort and concern involved in making the call. Address carer strain as an issue where needed.
### Management
Bring together the issues you have established. If safety requires action, take the lead; otherwise involve the caller in choosing the priority.
> “There's a lot of things going on there. Where do you think we should start first?”
Arrange direct patient contact or examination when needed. Agree who will do what, when review will happen and what should prompt earlier help.
## Colleague discussion
### Establish the starting point
- Who is the colleague and what is their role?
- Have they spoken to the patient about discussing the situation with you?
- What has been discussed with the patient so far?
- What are the facts?
- What has happened so far?
- Who is already involved?
### Safety and context
- Are there immediate safety issues requiring action, such as a recent fall or an unwell patient?
- Are there safeguarding concerns or difficulty coping safely at home?
- Are there ethical or medicolegal issues, such as a requested letter or form?
- Who lives with the patient?
- What support is available?
- Which clinical team members are involved?
- Which nonclinical services, social services or other agencies are involved?
### Double ICE — the colleague and the patient
- **Colleague's ideas:** “What did you think about everything that was going on?”
- **Patient's ideas:** “What did they think about what was going on?”
- **Colleague's concerns:** “What worries you about the situation?”
- **Patient's concerns:** “What worries the patient about the situation?”
- **Colleague's expectations:** “What did you think I could do to help?”
- **Patient's expectations:** “What do you think they thought that we could do to help this particular situation?”
### Support the colleague and agree responsibility
> “Look, gosh it's a difficult situation, I appreciate you coming to me, it must be really hard to handle this.”
- Is advice or signposting enough, or do you need to assess the patient or act yourself?
- Who will contact the patient, other professionals or services?
- What action is needed today?
- What escalation or safety-netting is needed?
- Does the patient need follow-up?
- Does the colleague need follow-up?
- “Is it worth us touching base tomorrow again just to see how things were once you've maybe done x y z?”
- Are there cues that the colleague is struggling with pressure, stress or burnout?
Agree specific actions and ownership rather than offering reassurance without a plan or taking over everything unnecessarily.
