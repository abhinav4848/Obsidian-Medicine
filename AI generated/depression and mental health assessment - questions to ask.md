# Depression and mental health assessment — questions to ask

Adapted from the [shared Mental Health Questions Review notes](https://chatgpt.com/share/6aceae56-05cc-83ed-b545-4614a6a318ed), which combined depression and mental health risk assessment material from Ardens screenshots. The questions below are conversational prompts, including supplementary wording, rather than a verbatim Ardens questionnaire.

Related: [[depression]], [[bipolar disorder]], [[psychosis]], [[suicide]], [[sca- consultation keys]].

> [!tip] Consultation flow
> Open → symptoms and function → relevant history → safety → support and safeguarding → shared plan and review. Adapt the order to the patient; assess immediate safety as soon as concerns emerge.

## 1. At the start: understand why they have come

- “How have you been feeling recently?”
- “Can you tell me what has been happening?”
- “When did this start? Was anything happening around that time?”
- “Is it there all the time, or does it come and go?”
- “Are things getting better, worse, or staying the same?”
- “Have you felt like this before?”
- “What do you think is causing this?”
- “What worries you most?”
- “What were you hoping we could help with today?”

## 2. When depression is possible: symptoms and impact

### Initial screening

Ask about the **past month**:

- “Have you often been feeling down, depressed or hopeless?”
- “Have you often had little interest or pleasure in doing things?”

### Explore a possible depressive episode

- **Mood:** “How much of the day do you feel low? How many days has this been happening?”
- **Interest:** “What do you usually enjoy? Are you still able to enjoy those things?”
- **Energy:** “How is your energy? Do ordinary tasks feel exhausting?”
- **Sleep:** “How are you sleeping? Trouble getting to sleep, waking early, or sleeping more than usual?”
- **Appetite/weight:** “Has your appetite or weight changed?”
- **Guilt/worthlessness:** “How do you feel about yourself? Have you been blaming yourself or feeling worthless?”
- **Concentration:** “How is your concentration? Is it harder to read, watch television or make decisions?”
- **Agitation/slowing:** “Have you felt unusually restless or slowed down? Has anyone else noticed a change?”
- **Death/safety:** “Have you felt that life is not worth living, or thought about ending your life?” Follow up directly using section 5.
- **Duration:** “Have these symptoms been present on most days for at least two weeks?”

If relevant, explore atypical features:

- “Does your mood lift when something good happens?”
- “Have you been sleeping or eating much more than usual?”
- “Do your arms or legs feel unusually heavy?”
- “Have you become particularly sensitive to rejection?”

### Function — ask even if symptoms sound mild

- “What does a typical day look like now?”
- “How is this affecting work or study?”
- “Are you managing washing, dressing, meals and looking after your home?”
- “Have you stopped seeing friends or family?”
- “Are you managing to look after anyone who depends on you?”
- “What are you struggling with now that you could manage before?”

### When using PHQ-2 or PHQ-9

Use the **past two weeks**, rather than the month used for initial identification. Ask how often each symptom occurred: not at all, several days, more than half the days, or nearly every day (0–3).

PHQ-2 covers low mood and loss of interest. For PHQ-9, also cover sleep, energy, appetite, negative feelings about oneself, concentration, observable slowing/restlessness, and thoughts of death or self-harm. Use the standard form for formal scoring.

- “How difficult have these problems made work, home life or getting on with people?”

A positive death/self-harm item needs direct safety questions; the total score does not establish intent or replace clinical assessment.

## 3. During the fuller history: previous episodes and alternative explanations

### Previous mental health problems and treatment

- “Have you had depression or other mental health difficulties before?”
- “Have you seen a mental health team or needed hospital treatment?”
- “Have you tried talking therapy or medication? What helped, and what caused problems?”
- “Are you receiving support or treatment at the moment?”
- “Has anyone in your family had significant mental health difficulties?”

### Before considering antidepressants: possible bipolar symptoms

- “Have you ever had a period of unusually high or irritable mood?”
- “Were you much more active, talkative or confident than usual?”
- “Did you need much less sleep without feeling tired?”
- “Did you spend more, take risks or do things that were out of character?”
- “How long did it last? Did other people notice? What consequences were there?”

Previous overactivity or disinhibition lasting **four days or more** warrants consideration of specialist assessment. [NICE CG185, 1.2.1](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg185/chapter/recommendations#recognising-and-managing-bipolar-disorder-in-adults-in-primary-care).

### Possible psychosis

- “Have you heard voices or sounds that other people cannot hear?”
- “Have you felt watched, followed or threatened?”
- “Have you had experiences or beliefs that people around you find difficult to understand?”
- If voices are present: “What do they say? Do they tell you to do anything? Do you feel you might act on that?”

### Other symptoms, physical health, medicines and substances

- “Have you been very anxious, having panic attacks, or struggling with repeated unwanted thoughts?”
- “Have there been distressing experiences, nightmares or flashbacks?”
- “Have you had difficulties with eating or body image?”
- “Are there physical symptoms or long-term conditions affecting how you feel?”
- “What medicines and supplements do you take? Has anything changed recently?”
- “How much alcohol do you drink? Do you use any recreational drugs?”
- “Have alcohol or drugs been involved when things felt worse or you acted impulsively?”

## 4. When there are thoughts of self-harm, or a recent episode

### Current thoughts and behaviour

- “Have you been thinking about harming yourself?”
- “Have you harmed yourself recently? What happened, and when?”
- “Have you taken anything or injured yourself? Have you had medical assessment or treatment?”
- “How often do these thoughts come? What tends to trigger them?”
- “What does harming yourself do for you, or what were you hoping would happen?”
- “At that time, did you want or expect to die?”

### Plans, access and ability to resist

- “Have you thought about how or when you might hurt yourself?”
- “Have you made any plans or preparations?”
- “Do you have access to anything you might use?”
- “Do you feel you might act on these thoughts now or soon?”
- “How able do you feel to resist the urge?”

### Previous episodes and coping

- “Has this happened before? When was the last episode?”
- “Did you need medical treatment?”
- “What helped you get through it previously?”
- “What do you usually do when the urge comes? Who can you contact?”

Recent injury or poisoning may require urgent physical care alongside mental health assessment. Explore suicidal intent separately; self-harm can occur with or without an intention to die.

## 5. When assessing suicide concerns

### Passive wishes and active thoughts

- “Have you felt that life is not worth living?”
- “Have you wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?”
- “Have you felt that others would be better off without you?”
- **“Have you thought about ending your own life?”**
- “When did this start? How often does it happen?”
- “Are the thoughts becoming stronger or harder to control?”

### Plans, preparations, means and intent

- “Have you thought about how or when you might end your life?”
- “Have you made a plan or taken any steps to prepare?”
- “Do you have access to the things you have considered using?”
- “Have you put your affairs in order or made other preparations?”
- “Do you intend to act on these thoughts? Do you think you might act today or soon?”
- “How able do you feel to resist acting?”
- “What has helped you hold on so far?”

### Previous attempts and present support

- “Have you ever tried to take your own life?”
- “When was that? What happened? Did you need hospital treatment?”
- “What did you expect or intend to happen at the time?”
- “Who knows how you are feeling?”
- “Who could be with you or help you today?”
- “What would help you feel safer now?”

Passive wishes, active thoughts, plans and intent are distinct; they are not a fixed sequence every patient follows. Reasons for living help with care planning but do not cancel immediate safety concerns.

## 6. When there are concerns about harm to others

- “Have you had thoughts about hurting someone else?”
- “Are these thoughts about a particular person?”
- “How often do they happen? Have you threatened anyone recently?”
- “Have you made a plan or prepared anything? Do you have access to what you would use?”
- “Do you intend to act? Have you thought about when?”
- “How able do you feel to control the urge?”
- “Is anyone else worried you might hurt someone?”
- “Have you harmed someone before? What happened?”
- “Have the police been involved in the past or recently?”
- “Are voices telling you to hurt someone, or do you feel you need to defend yourself?”
- “Have alcohol or drugs been involved?”
- “Are children or vulnerable adults around, or potentially affected?”

Clarify the target, intent, access, preparations and immediacy, then act on the individual safety and safeguarding needs.

## 7. Throughout the assessment: circumstances, support and safeguarding

### Stressors and support

- “Who do you live with? How are things at home?”
- “Have there been losses, relationship problems, money worries, work pressures or housing difficulties?”
- “Do you feel isolated? Who can you talk to?”
- “What has helped you cope before?”
- “What matters to you, or gives you something to look forward to?”
- “What makes it difficult to ask for help or attend appointments?”

### Safeguarding and basic needs

- “Do you feel safe at home?”
- “Is anyone hurting, threatening, controlling or exploiting you?”
- “Are you able to eat, drink and look after yourself?”
- “Are there children or vulnerable adults who depend on you? Who is looking after them now?”
- “Is there anyone else's safety you are worried about?”

Observe the **mental state examination** throughout: appearance, behaviour, speech, mood/affect, thoughts, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. Summarise the person's difficulties, history, changing circumstances, support and immediate safety needs. Do not use a score or a global low/medium/high label to predict suicide or repeat self-harm. [NICE NG225, 1.6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225/chapter/Recommendations#risk-assessment-tools-and-scales).

## 8. Before closing: agree treatment and a practical safety plan

### Shared treatment decisions

- “What would you most like to improve first?”
- “What helped previously? How do you feel about talking therapy or medication?”
- “What concerns do you have about the options?”
- “Is there anything that would make this plan difficult to follow?”
- “Would you be comfortable involving someone you trust or your existing mental health team?”

### Safety planning

- “What warning signs tell you things are getting worse?”
- “What can you do to get through a difficult moment?”
- “Who or where could you turn to for support or distraction?”
- “How could we make your surroundings safer and reduce access to things you might use to harm yourself?”
- “Who can help with that today?”
- “Which professional service will you contact if things worsen, including outside normal hours?”
- “Can we write the plan down so you can find it easily?”
- “Can you talk me through what you would do if you felt unable to stay safe?”

Make the plan specific and collaborative. A promise not to self-harm is not a substitute for a safety plan. [NICE NG225, 1.11.7–1.11.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225/chapter/Recommendations#interventions-for-self-harm).

**Immediate danger requires urgent assessment and a supported route to care; do not defer this to the routine review intervals below.**

## 9. At follow-up: what to ask at each time

These intervals apply to adult depression and the specified self-harm circumstances. Adapt timing to clinical need.

| Situation | Timing | Main focus |
|---|---|---|
| Antidepressant started or dose increased in someone aged 18–25, or with increased suicide risk | **1 week** | Suicidality, agitation, side effects and safety plan; further reviews as needed, no later than 4 weeks after starting |
| Other patients starting an antidepressant | Usually **within 2 weeks** | Symptoms, tolerability, adherence and safety |
| Less severe depression with active monitoring | Normally **2–4 weeks** | Progress, function, safety and whether treatment is now wanted |
| Review of depression treatment response | **2–4 weeks** after starting | Benefit, adherence, adverse effects and ongoing safety |
| Self-harm with ongoing safety concerns after psychosocial assessment | Initial aftercare **within 48 hours of that assessment** | Current safety, physical needs, support and the agreed care plan |

Timing: [NICE NG222, 1.4.3, 1.4.11, 1.4.22 and 1.5.1](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng222/chapter/Recommendations), and [NICE NG225, 1.10.2](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225/chapter/Recommendations#initial-aftercare-after-an-episode-of-self-harm).

### Ask at every relevant review

- “Since we last spoke, what has improved, worsened or stayed the same?”
- “How are sleep, appetite, energy, enjoyment and day-to-day functioning?”
- “Have you had thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life? Any change in plans, access or intent?”
- “Have you felt more restless, agitated, impulsive, unusually energetic or unable to sleep?”
- “Have you been able to take the medication as agreed? Any missed doses, side effects or worries?”
- “Have you been able to access the talking therapy or other support?”
- “Has anything changed at home or in your support network?”
- “Is the safety plan usable? What needs updating?”
- “What would you like us to change or focus on next?”

### At early aftercare following self-harm

- “Have you harmed yourself again or felt close to doing so?”
- “Are there injuries or physical symptoms that still need attention?”
- “Have the agreed steps to reduce access to means happened?”
- “Who has been supporting you? Have you been able to contact the services in your plan?”
- “What has been hardest since the assessment, and what help do you need now?”

> [!question]- Quick SCA recall
> **Symptoms → function → history/differentials → self-harm/suicide → others/safeguarding → support → shared plan → review.**
> If safety concerns emerge, address them immediately rather than finishing the sequence first.
